Abstract. Quantum physics and gravity are two different concepts which need to be unified in a widely
acceptable way. While electromagnetism, weak nuclear force, and strong nuclear force were accurately
unified under the framework of quantum field theory, gravity remains elusive and couldn’t be unified with
quantum mechanics. However, gravity is accurately understood by the theory of relativity, which is not
complementing quantum physics. This elusiveness is due to the missing consciousness dimension in the
mathematical frameworks of fundamental physics, which can explain the causation of gravity, quantum
mechanics, and reality. Intricately interweaving the dimension of consciousness with the mathematical
frameworks of fundamental physics can make our understanding of reality complete.

1. Introduction
When understanding reality and how things behave in nature, causation plays a crucial role. Some
believe causation is primitive, basic or fundamental, while others argue it is derivative, a construct or a
byproduct. As per David Hume and Simon Blackburn [1], causation is patterns composed by the human
mind in response to regularities in nature. From their viewpoint, the explanation of the cause and effect
is unknown, but how things behave can be analysed. Further, causation is not part of reality but a human
construct that depends on the laws of nature. In contrast, Richard Swinburne [2] argues causation is a
fundamental of reality. Causation cannot be explained by anything else, such as patterns, regularities
or laws in nature. Similarly, Robin Le Poidevin [3] describes causation as primitive, which cannot be
explained by other constructs or concepts. In fact, the other concept can be explained by causation. Most
notably, he argued that time is derived from a change and attributed to cause and effect. That is, to the
direction of cause and effect, time flows. However, Huw Price [4] argues that laws of physics do not
explain the direction of time, and backward causality is also possible.
In the laws of physics, the notion of time appears as a basis for the progression of states from the past
to the present into the future. As per Newtonian physics [5], there exists an absolute time independent
from an observer, and it flows from past to future at a consistent pace throughout the universe. However,
later based on the theory of relativity [6], Einstein showed that time is relative to the observer, where
that is the speed of light (not the time), same for any reference frame. Time emerges from an awareness
of change where both change and awareness are important to perceive time. The change underpins
causation which implies cause and effect following the laws of thermodynamics. As per the second
law of thermodynamics, the total entropy of a process will not decrease. Therefore, entropy gives a
physical meaning to the direction of time, sometimes called an arrow of time. Moreover, from relativity,

It is not only physical states, but the neural and mental states are also important to understand
causation. In fact, the idea of mental causation—the mind’s causal interaction with the physical
world and behaviour—is a quite old established philosophy evolving even today. About 2500 years
ago, the Buddha stated (in Pali), “Manō pubbangamā dhammā - manō setthā manōmayā”, which
means the mind underpins causation and the mind is supreme. The Buddha referred to the causation
as paticca-samuppada, which is discussed in detail in Patthana Pakarana in Abhidhamma Pitakaya

mental terminology. The interrelation of mental, neural and physical states describes different levels of
causation.
The observer and the awareness are discussed in both relativity and quantum mechanics. In relativity,
the notion of time on causation is relativistic and depends on the speed and gravity (i.e., the curvature
of space-time) [10]. On the other hand, quantum mechanics imposes limitations on awareness of the
physical world. As per Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle [11], it is impossible to be aware of both
the position and momentum (or certain pairs of physical quantities) of a particle with perfect accuracy.
Eugene Wigner suggested that consciousness interacts with causation at the quantum level. He stated that
consciousness causes the collapse of the wave function to experience the quantum reality—the quantum
level observation or measurement [12]. Recent discoveries suggest that awareness can be faster than
light exceeding physical limitations when quantum entanglement creates non-local reality [13, 14, 15].
Because of such developments in fundamental physics, some scholars now hypothesise consciousness as
fundamental [16].
The causation of consciousness is still a contemporary research topic. Some believe consciousness
is caused by brain function. Roger Penrose initially suggested that consciousness does not correlate
with the brain’s neural function [17]. He argued that a large portion of brain neurons (i.e., the
Cerebellum contains ∼80% of the brain’s neurons) are not contributing to consciousness. However,
later, Roger Penrose supported the hypothesis that consciousness emerges from quantum vibrations in the
orchestrated collapse of microtubules inside brain neurons [18]. This idea may unify the consciousness
with neuroscience and quantum mechanics. In contrast, Artificial Intelligence (AI) imitating the brain’s
neural computation [19, 20] aka deep neural networks (e.g., convolution neural networks (CNN),
recurrent neural networks (RNN), transformer models) has achieved impressive developments related to
human cognitive functions (e.g., image processing, natural language processing (NLP)). These artificial
neural networks proved their ability to self-learn and adapt, which is studied in reinforcement learning—a
form of machine learning in which an AI agent learns by trial and error sequentially [21]. On the other
hand, McFadden posits that consciousness is underpinned by the brain’s energy field [22]. He suggested
that electromagnetic energy correlates with consciousness and interplay with reality.
The hard problem of consciousness [23] articulates how perceptions or subjective experience (e.g.,
colour, taste, shapes, etc.) map into objective measurements like neural activities in a brain. The recent
research on neuroscience, cognitive models and neural correlates of consciousness tries to understand
how perception and consciousness are formed in the brain. On the other hand, the observable reality is
about 5%, and the rest of the universe consists of dark matter and dark energy, which cannot be observed
but interact with reality. Moreover, proof of non-local reality suggests that awareness is beyond physical
limitations, such as the speed of light. Being aware of dark matter, dark energy and non-local reality
might require driving consciousness beyond our sensory perception (i.e., touch, sight, hearing, smell and
taste).

3.2. Understand Consciousness and Change as the Foundation of Fundamental Physics
Fundamental physics can be understood either by relativity or quantum physics. These two fundamental
frameworks are not complementing each other. In relativity, we understand reality based on the
interaction of mass and energy in space-time. In relativity, both position and momentum are certain.
However, when the dimensions of the subject get smaller, that is, either in space or time, reality cannot
be understood by relativity anymore, where we need quantum mechanics to explain reality. If we look
into the main difference between these two frameworks, the uncertainty of the observation becomes
obvious.
The uncertainty of an observation is related to the consciousness of an observer and the complexity
of the phenomena. Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle [11] describes that it is impossible to be aware of
multiple physical quantities or complex scenarios with perfect accuracy at the quantum scale. The more
awareness, the less uncertain, and the less awareness, there will be more uncertainty. Awareness has its
limits, and like everything discreet, awareness is also discreet and has the shortest duration to be aware
of the change in reality. This shortest time is what was defined as the consciousness cycle, the shortest
duration that can be perceived in reality. On the other hand, change is a fundamental attribute of reality
that brings uncertainty to the observation. In a complex phenomenon, it might be difficult to sample the
change with consciousness precisely. Therefore, uncertainty cannot be avoided at the quantum scale, and
the quantum nature of reality emerges. In contrast, when the subject is reasonably large in space-time,
the observer can reasonably be aware of the behaviour of the subject where uncertainty is irrelevant. In
summary, awareness is one of the important concepts for our understanding of reality and an effect of
consciousness and change. Observers’ consciousness and the complexity of the phenomena determine
what can be aware and observed.

3.3. Understand the Gravity through the Consciousness

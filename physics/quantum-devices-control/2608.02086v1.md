Parasitic two-level-system (TLS) defects are a major source of energy relaxation and temporal instability in superconducting quantum processors. Our sub-second adaptive spectroscopy reveals telegraphic switching of TLSs with a characteristic timescale of a few seconds and spectral diffusion with diffusivity  $D \approx 0.9\,\mathrm{MHz^2\,s^{-1}}$ . These timescales are about  $3\times10^2$  times faster than what is observed in conventional nonadaptive spectroscopy, which typically requires hours of measurement time. We resolve such fast dynamics on a field-programmable gate array (FPGA)-based controller that enables measurement of frequency- and time-resolved relaxations with sub-second temporal resolution in flux-tunable superconducting qubits. We observe similar defect dynamics across multiple qubits in independently fabricated devices measured in different laboratories. We correlate TLS-induced fluctuations with gate-level errors using randomized benchmarking. Our results reveal a previously inaccessible regime of frequency-resolved TLS dynamics and redefine the timescales relevant to TLS-aware characterization and calibration of superconducting quantum processors.

### I. INTRODUCTION

Two-level system (TLS) defects are a main source of decoherence in superconducting qubits [1–3] and semiconductor spin qubits [4–7], although their detailed microscopic origins remain unknown. An individual defect can cause discrete telegraphic jumps in the qubit frequency when dispersively coupled or modulate the qubit energy-decay rate  $\Gamma_1$  when nearly resonant with the qubit energy splitting [8, 9]. Ensembles of TLSs can also produce approximately 1/f charge or flux noise from sub-Hz to kHz frequencies. These fluctuations degrade the fidelity of quantum operations and constitute non-Markovian noise [10] that can compromise quantum error correction [11] and increase the cost of error mitigation [12]. As the size of quantum processing units (QPUs) increases, the probability that at least one qubit is limited by a TLS grows exponentially. Fast, time-dependent fluctuations make it nontrivial to identify which qubits limit the QPU performance at any given moment. Moreover, individual TLSs can fluctuate in frequency within a cooldown and rearrange across different cooldowns, even though the total defect count remains approximately unchanged [13, 14].

TLSs distributed across the GHz spectrum, including defects in aluminum oxide and other insulating materials, can interact strongly with superconducting qubits through electric-dipole coupling [13, 15]. Strategies for reducing TLS-induced errors include improvements in device architecture, materials, and fabrication [2, 16–19]; direct control or stabilization of noise sources [15, 20–25]; and real-time feedback [26].

Switches in relaxation time by an order of magnitude have been observed over timescales of just milliseconds [27]. In fixed-frequency relaxation tracking, only a small fraction of such rapid  $\Gamma_1(t)$  fluctuations can be attributed to TLS-like dynamics, because the qubit frequency is fixed and the underlying defect cannot be tracked spectrally. Flux-tunable gubits instead provide the spectral degree of freedom needed to map the frequency dynamics of TLS resonances that cause rapid relaxation-time fluctuations. Previous works in transmon qubits have reported TLS spectral diffusion on timescales of hours [17, 28–31]. Accessing faster regimes of TLS dvnamics requires estimation protocols that resolve the dynamics before they are averaged out and can rapidly flag outlier qubits and time-dependent fluctuations [1-3, 16] in large QPUs. Such information can support efficient characterization and error mitigation [32, 33] by identifying and avoiding frequencies where TLSs are resonant

Here we extend the adaptive decay-rate estimation method of Ref. [27], demonstrated in fixed-frequency transmon qubits, to frequency-resolved TLS spectroscopy with flux-tunable transmons. This work is organized around three parts: (i) we describe how FPGA-based adaptive estimates of  $\Gamma_1$  are converted into time-resolved TLS maps, (ii) we quantify the seconds-scale telegraphic and diffusive motion revealed by these maps, and (iii) we interleave the same spectroscopy with randomized benchmarking to test whether the tracked relaxation-rate fluctuations are visible at the gate-error level.

### II. PROTOCOL

We implement the adaptive relaxation-spectroscopy protocol on two superconducting-qubit devices, Device A and Device B, containing transmons with DC SQUIDs [35, 36]. Device A consists of uncoupled flux-tunable transmons, whereas Device B has flux-tunable transmons connected by tunable couplers. The devices are fabricated with different processes and are measured in two different laboratories: Device A at the Israeli Quantum Computing Center (IQCC) and Device B at MIT. The qubits have maximum frequencies near 5.1 GHz in Device A and 6.2 GHz in Device B. A commercial controller with integrated FPGA hardware (Quantum Machines OPX1000) synthesizes the high-frequency waveforms applied to the on-chip Z and XY control lines and demodulates the resonator readout signals. The Supplemental Material provides further details on the experimental setups and device fabrication [37].

Flux-biased TLS spectroscopy estimates the decay rate  $\Gamma_1$  while tuning the qubit frequency  $f_{\rm q}$  with the external flux  $\Phi$  [see Fig. 1(a)]. When the qubit is resonant with the defect, energy is transferred to the TLS, which subsequently relaxes into its local environment, giving rise to an enhanced qubit decay rate. Bayesian parameter estimation provides a natural strategy for low-latency adaptive estimation and control [27, 38–45]. Rather than storing only a point estimate, the controller keeps track of a probability distribution for  $\Gamma_1$  and uses it to choose the next probe wait time after each single-shot measurement. Here we use the protocol of Ref. [27] to estimate  $\Gamma_1$  more than  $100\times$  faster than conventional decay measurements, enabling fast, frequency-resolved spectroscopy of TLSs.

For each applied flux bias  $\Phi_j$   $(1 \leq j \leq M)$ , corresponding to a qubit frequency  $f_{\mathbf{q}}(\Phi_j)$ , the controller estimates  $\Gamma_1$  from N adaptive probe cycles  $(1 \leq i \leq N)$ , as schematized in Fig. 1(b). At the start of probe cycle i, a conditional  $X_{\pi}$  pulse prepares the qubit in  $|1\rangle$  at  $\Phi = 0$ . The qubit is then tuned to the target frequency for the adaptive wait time  $\tau_i$  (described below), returned to  $\Phi = 0$ , after which dispersive readout returns  $m_i \in \{0,1\}$ , corresponding to the states  $|0\rangle$  and  $|1\rangle$ . Each single-shot outcome updates the probability distribution for  $\Gamma_1$ .

In the quasistatic approximation, Bayes' rule gives  $\mathcal{P}_{i+1}(\Gamma_1) \propto \mathcal{P}_i(\Gamma_1)P(m_{i+1}|\Gamma_1,\tau_{i+1}),$  where the prior  $\mathcal{P}_i(\Gamma_1)$  is the probability distribution for  $\Gamma_1$  after the  $i^{\rm th}$  probe cycle and therefore depends on all previous wait times and measurement outcomes. The likelihood  $P(m_{i+1}|\Gamma_1,\tau_{i+1})$  is the probability of measuring outcome  $m_{i+1}$  after initialization and waiting for  $\tau_{i+1}$  [46], and the posterior  $\mathcal{P}_{i+1}(\Gamma_1)$  is the updated distribution after the subsequent cycle. Operationally, the key idea is that the probability distribution is stored and updated on the controller using only two parameters, as detailed in Ref. [27]. The updated estimate  $\hat{\Gamma}_1$  sets the next wait time,  $\tau_{i+1} = c/\hat{\Gamma}_1$ , with c = 0.1. After each final  $\hat{\Gamma}_1$  estimate, the prior is reinitialized so that estimates at different flux points and laboratory times are independent. Repeating this adaptive estimate over qubit frequency and laboratory time yields the telegraphic TLS map in Fig. 1(c), which is a zoom-in of the data in Fig. 2(a).

### III. RESULTS

# A. Fast time-resolved spectroscopy of defect-induced relaxation

We first apply our protocol to Device A to probe TLSs as a function of qubit frequency with previously unattainable temporal resolution. Figure 2 presents the main TLS maps: Low-latency spectroscopy reveals TLS-induced decay-rate peaks that switch and diffuse on timescales of seconds.

Figure 2(a) shows the controller estimates  $\hat{\Gamma}_1$  in  $Q_1$ . The controller estimates  $\Gamma_1$  using N=30 probe cycles [47] at each value of  $\Phi$ , sweeping the flux so that  $f_{Q1}$  spans a 500 MHz detuning range from the upper degeneracy point in 1 MHz steps. Each  $\Gamma_1$  estimate requires only about 1.5 ms of laboratory time. Every frequency sweep is interleaved with a Ramsey experiment at  $\Phi=0$  to verify afterwards that the qubit frequency is calibrated within  $\pm 1$  MHz.

The most striking feature in Fig. 2(a) is the pair of anticorrelated decay-rate peaks highlighted by the dashed

## PDF page 4

lines. Their anticorrelation suggests that a single resonant TLS switches between two discrete transition frequencies through interactions with another TLS, producing telegraphic fluctuations of the qubit relaxation rate [28]. From a Bayesian analysis based on a two-state hidden Markov model (see Supplemental Material [37]), we obtain the characteristic switching correlation time  $\tau_{\rm sw} = 2.20^{+0.24}_{-0.22}\,\rm s$ , where the quoted value is the posterior median and the uncertainty is the 68% credible interval. In contrast to fixed-frequency tracking [27], flux tunability lets us identify the spectral motion associated with these relaxation events.

The rapid switching events in Fig. 2(a) are spectroscopically resolved only because each frequency sweep is acquired with a sub-second sampling period. By comparison, previous transmon TLS and coherence-tracking experiments [9, 28, 30] typically used sampling intervals of tens of minutes, approximately two orders of magnitude longer than ours, making such second-scale TLS dynamics not directly observable. Additional horizontal peaks are shown in  $\Gamma_1$  of Fig. 2(a), which may be associated with other TLSs or modes in the setup. Thus, while slow drifts still require long measurements, our protocol makes it possible to acquire two-dimensional spectroscopy maps with sufficient statistics to characterize fast TLS fluctuations within minutes.

Panels 2(b) and (c) show zoomed scans from two other qubits on the same device, acquired with similar settings (wider scans are included in the Supplemental Material [37]). In panel (b), the highlighted TLS feature undergoes a diffusive process [28]. We extract its center frequency  $f_{TLS}(t)$ , compute the mean-squared displacement  $MSD(\tau) = \langle [f_{TLS}(t+\tau) - f_{TLS}(t)]^2 \rangle_t$ , and fit the result to an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck diffusion model. In the shortlag limit, this model gives  $MSD(\tau) \approx 2D\tau$ , where D is the diffusivity, and  $\tau$  is the lag time. The diffusivity sets the rate at which the TLS frequency wanders. From the fit we obtain  $D=0.93^{+0.18}_{-0.22}\,\mathrm{MHz^2\,s^{-1}},$  where the uncertainty tainty is the 68% interval from a block bootstrap resampling of the tracked trajectory (see the Supplemental Material [37]). After converting previously reported diffusivities to the same short-time MSD convention, this diffusivity is about  $3 \times 10^2$  times larger than values reported from conventional nonadaptive spectroscopy [17, 28]. Panel (c) illustrates a mixed regime, where slow, approximately diffusive drift is superimposed on fast telegraphic switching. Additional telegraphic jumps are also visible at higher frequencies in panels (b) and (c).

## B. TLS-correlated gate-error fluctuations

To assess whether the fast fluctuations of a TLS impact gate performance, we interleave adaptive estimates of  $\Gamma_1$  at the operating point marked in Fig. 3(a) with single-qubit randomized benchmarking (RB) [48] to estimate the native gate infidelity  $r_{\rm g}$ . As described in the inset of Fig. 3(b), each RB estimate of the average gate infidelity  $r_{\rm g}$  is interleaved with a  $\Gamma_1$  estimate. Each  $r_{\rm g}$  estimate is obtained from 300 random sequences, with circuit depths stepped logarithmically in powers of two up to 2048, while each  $\Gamma_1$  estimate uses N=40 probe cycles. The average sampling time is approximately 0.186 s

and is largely dominated by the RB estimation time.

In the main panel of Fig. 3(b), we plot 5 s moving averages of the gate infidelity extracted offline from RB (gray curve) and of the infidelity expected from relaxation (purple curve), approximated as  $t_{\rm gate}\Gamma_1/3$  [49] with  $t_{\rm gate}\approx 20\,{\rm ns}$ . The shaded regions represent 68% confidence intervals for the moving averages, and they are barely visible because of small uncertainties. For the RB curve, the interval is computed from the standard error of the fitted gate infidelities within the averaging window. For the  $\Gamma_1$ -limited curve, the posterior standard deviations of  $\Gamma_1$  are combined over the same averaging window.

The RB infidelity follows the relaxation-limited estimate at the level of slow temporal trends, suggesting that the TLS contributes to the observed gate-error fluctuations. Quantitatively, the normalized zero-lag correlation between the 5s moving-averaged RB infidelity and the corresponding relaxation-limited estimate is  $\rho_0 = 0.618$ . To verify that this correlation is associated with the tracked TLS rather than a generic background fluctuation, we repeat the experiment while biasing the qubit frequency a few MHz away from the TLS resonance. In this off-TLS setting, the correlation drops to  $\rho_0$  = -0.0507 (see the Supplemental Material [37]). We therefore interpret the tracked TLS as a significant, but not exclusive, contributor to the observed gate-error fluctuations. We tentatively attribute the remaining discrepancies to dephasing-related fluctuations and other RBsensitive noise sources not captured by  $\Gamma_1$  alone.

### IV. DISCUSSION AND OUTLOOK

The main result of this work is the observation of spectral diffusion of TLSs on timescales of seconds. For the telegraphic feature in Fig. 2(a), the Bayesian hidden Markov model analysis gives a nominal switching time  $\tau_{\rm sw}\approx 2.2\,{\rm s}$ . For the diffusive feature in Fig. 2(b), the spectral-diffusion analysis gives  $D\approx 0.93\,{\rm MHz^2\,s^{-1}}$ , about  $3\times 10^2$  times larger than previously reported by conventional TLS spectroscopy [17, 28]. Our findings are enabled by adaptive Bayesian estimation in flux-tunable transmon qubits: Frequency sweeps combined with low-latency FPGA updates produce time-resolved TLS maps with sub-second sampling over a wide frequency range.

Interleaved randomized benchmarking shows that the observed TLS dynamics are correlated with fluctuations of gate performance. Because the  $\Gamma_1$  enhancement correlated with the TLS is estimated online on the controller and requires only a runtime on the order of tens of  $1/\Gamma_1$ , our protocol could be integrated into adaptive control loops with low-latency for error mitigation. For example, spectroscopy could be interleaved with circuit execution and used to temporarily pause operation when the estimated relaxation rate is above a user-defined threshold.

Our approach is therefore useful for rapid qubit characterization, device-level process feedback, and calibra-

tion strategies that account for frequency- and time-dependent TLS fluctuations. Looking ahead, the same framework can be combined with controlled TLS tuning (electric field or strain) [15, 20–24], mapping the positions of TLSs [50], AC Stark modulation [23, 30], and probes of background ionizing radiation [51].

While improvements in design, materials, and fabrication are ongoing and worth pursuing, our results motivate a shift from periodic recalibration every few hours to low-latency calibration loops [44, 52]. By identifying TLS dynamics as a source of rapid spectral and temporal fluctuations of the decay rate, our work provides a better understanding of microscopic defects to improve QPU performance.

### II. TELEGRAPHIC SWITCHING ANALYSIS

We analyze the telegraphic TLS dynamics in Fig. [S2](#page-11-1) using the same dataset as Fig. 2(a) of the main text. As in the spectral-diffusion analysis below, the measured relaxation time is first converted to a relaxation rate,

$$\Gamma_1(f_i, t_n) = \frac{1}{T_1(f_i, t_n)}.$$
(S.1)

The map is smoothed with a Gaussian kernel of width one pixel along the time and frequency axes. We then subtract the static frequency-dependent background,

$$\Gamma_1^{\text{dyn}}(f_i, t_n) = \Gamma_1^{\text{sm}}(f_i, t_n) - \text{median}_n[\Gamma_1^{\text{sm}}(f_i, t_n)], \qquad (S.2)$$

and restrict the analysis to the frequency interval from −340 MHz to −180 MHz containing the telegraphic feature. The switching trajectory is extracted as a two-state process. We define two frequency bands corresponding to the observed TLS states,

$$B_0 = [-335 \,\text{MHz}, -285 \,\text{MHz}], \qquad B_1 = [-230 \,\text{MHz}, -185 \,\text{MHz}].$$
 (S.3)

For each laboratory time t<sup>n</sup> and band Bk, we compute a band score xk(tn) from the mean of the ten largest values of the positive dynamic signal inside the band,


$$x_k(t_n) = \frac{1}{N_{\text{top}}} \sum_{j \in \text{top}(B_k, N_{\text{top}})} \max \left[ \Gamma_1^{\text{dyn}}(f_j, t_n) - q_{10}(t_n), 0 \right], \tag{S.4}$$

## PDF page 13

where  $N_{\text{top}} = 10$  and  $q_{10}(t_n)$  is the 10th percentile of  $\Gamma_1^{\text{dyn}}(f, t_n)$  within the analyzed frequency interval. From these scores we extract the switching rate in three steps: a hysteresis decoding of the two-state trajectory, a hidden-Markov analysis that propagates the state-assignment uncertainty, and an Allan-deviation cross-check of the resulting rates.

### A. Hysteresis filtering

We now assign the observed signal  $\mathbf{x}_n = [x_0(t_n), x_1(t_n)]$  to a hidden state of two-level fluctuator  $s_n \in \{0, 1\}$ . The simplest assignment applies a hysteresis rule to the score difference: a state switch is accepted only when the difference between the signals exceeds its own scatter. In particular, we define the score difference as

$$\Delta \overline{x}(t_n) = \overline{x}_1(t_n) - \overline{x}_0(t_n), \tag{S.5}$$

where we additionally smooth each score over a short window,  $\overline{x}_k(t_n) = \frac{1}{w} \sum ...$ , with w = 3 samples ( $\approx 2.29$  s). Next we compare against the finite threshold  $h = 0.1 \times \text{std}(\Delta \overline{x})$ . We assume the hidden state  $s_n \in \{0,1\}$  switches from 0 to 1 only when  $\Delta \overline{x}(t_n) > h$ , and from 1 to 0 only when  $\Delta \overline{x}(t_n) < -h$ . The plotted trajectory is then


$$f_{\text{TLS}}(t_n) = \frac{1}{2} \left[ f_{\min}(B_{s_n}) + f_{\max}(B_{s_n}) \right].$$
 (S.6)

Thus the red curve in Fig. S2 reports the assigned discrete state. The raw band scores have a correlation coefficient  $\rho = -0.36$ , strengthening to  $\rho = -0.52$  when the positive signal is integrated over all frequencies in each band (Fig. S3). This anticorrelation supports the interpretation of spectral-weight transfer between the two assigned frequency states, but it does not by itself prove exact conservation of the integrated loss amplitude.

Counting transitions directly on this decoded trajectory gives 106 switches, state populations (0.52, 0.48), and rates  $k_{01} = 0.134 \,\mathrm{Hz}$ ,  $k_{10} = 0.144 \,\mathrm{Hz}$ , so that  $1/(k_{01} + k_{10}) = 3.59 \,\mathrm{s}$ . This hard-threshold count returns a single value with no uncertainty and is biased toward long dwell times: the score smoothing merges rapid back-and-forth switches below the temporal resolution, so many short dwells are likely omitted from the count and the inferred rate is underestimated. Establishing the switching rate therefore requires a model-based analysis that propagates the state-assignment ambiguity rather than committing to a single decoded path, which we develop next.

### B. Bayesian estimation of the switching rates

The above analysis establishes telegraphic switching but returns a single decoded trajectory, and hence a single value per rate with no statistical uncertainty. We therefore add a hidden Markov model (HMM) [2] that propagates two sources of uncertainty: the ambiguity of the state assignment at each time step, and the finite-sample noise of counting a limited number of switches. By assigning probabilities to paths rather than committing to a single decoding, it recovers the short dwells that the hysteresis count merges and returns a full posterior for each rate. The hidden state is the TLS state  $s_n \in \{0,1\}$ , and the observation is the band-score vector  $\mathbf{x}_n = [\overline{x}_0(t_n), \overline{x}_1(t_n)]$  of Eq. (S.4), standardized per component. Conditioned on the state, the observation is a full-covariance Gaussian,


$$p(\mathbf{x}_n \mid s_n = k) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n; \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k), \tag{S.7}$$

so each state is a Gaussian cluster in the two-dimensional score plane (Fig. S4). The hidden state evolves through a discrete transition matrix at spacing  $\Delta t$ ,

$$A_{\text{fit}}(i,j) = P(s_{n+1} = j \mid s_n = i), \qquad i, j \in \{0,1\}.$$
 (S.8)

The emission parameters  $\{\mu_k, \Sigma_k\}$  and  $A_{\text{fit}}$  are estimated *jointly* from the sequence  $\{\mathbf{x}_n\}$  by Baum–Welch expectation maximization [3], which maximizes the data log-likelihood  $\mathcal{L} = \log \sum_i \alpha_T(i)$  with  $\alpha$  the forward quantity of Eq. (S.9); we use full covariances and 500 EM iterations. This replaces the fixed threshold and hysteresis rule of Eq. (S.6) by a learned, probabilistic state assignment. For this dataset the two fitted clusters are well separated (Fig. S4), and the most probable trajectory (Viterbi algorithm) of the fitted model agrees with the hysteresis trajectory at the 97% level.

## 1. Forward-filtering backward-sampling

# C. Allan deviation

As an independent check of the hidden-Markov rates that does not rely on a spectral fit, we examine the overlapping Allan deviation computed from the single trajectory and from trajectories sampled from the rate posterior. For an averaging time  $\tau_m = m \Delta t$ , the discrete moving average is

$$\overline{f}_{n,m} = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1} f_{\text{TLS}}(t_{n+\ell}), \tag{S.15}$$

and the overlapping Allan deviation is


$$\sigma_f(\tau_m) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2} \langle \left(\overline{f}_{n+m,m} - \overline{f}_{n,m}\right)^2 \rangle_n}.$$
 (S.16)

A two-state Markov (telegraphic) process has a single correlation time, and hence a single Lorentzian spectrum; its Allan deviation therefore rises as  $\sqrt{\tau}$  at short averaging times, peaks near the switching time, and falls as  $1/\sqrt{\tau}$  afterward. The measured  $\sigma_f(\tau)$  shows this single-humped shape (Fig. S7), indicating a single few-second timescale rather than a distribution of rates or a 1/f background.

#### III. SPECTRAL DIFFUSION ANALYSIS

To quantify the spectral diffusion of the fluctuating TLS highlighted in Fig. S8(a), same as Fig.2(b) of the main text, we first convert the measured relaxation time to a relaxation rate.

$$\Gamma_1(f_i, t_n) = \frac{1}{T_1(f_i, t_n)}.$$
(S.17)

We then smooth the map with a Gaussian kernel of width one pixel along both the time and frequency axes, and subtract the time-independent background at each frequency point,

$$\Gamma_1^{\text{dyn}}(f_i, t_n) = \Gamma_1^{\text{sm}}(f_i, t_n) - \text{median}_n[\Gamma_1^{\text{sm}}(f_i, t_n)].$$
(S.18)

This subtraction suppresses static horizontal features in the spectroscopy map and leaves the time-dependent component of the loss feature.

The TLS trajectory is extracted from a frequency window around the feature using a Viterbi tracker. Let  $S_{n,j} = \Gamma_1^{\text{dyn}}(f_j, t_n)$  be the dynamic signal in this window. The tracked frequency-index sequence  $\{j_n\}$  is chosen to maximize

$$\mathcal{L}(\{j_n\}) = \sum_{n=1}^{N_t} S_{n,j_n} - \lambda \sum_{n=2}^{N_t} |j_n - j_{n-1}|,$$
(S.19)

with a jump penalty  $\lambda = 10$ . The resulting frequency trajectory is  $f_{TLS}(t_n) = f_{j_n}$ .

We analyze the trajectory using both its power spectral density and mean-squared displacement. For the power spectral density shown in Fig. S8(b), we subtract the mean frequency and estimate

$$S_f(\nu) = \frac{1}{T} \left| \int_0^T \left[ f_{\text{TLS}}(t) - \langle f_{\text{TLS}} \rangle \right] e^{-i2\pi\nu t} dt \right|^2,$$
 (S.20)

using Welch averaging in the numerical implementation.

The mean-squared displacement is computed from the discrete trajectory as


$$MSD(\tau_m) = \frac{1}{N_t - m} \sum_{n=1}^{N_t - m} \left[ f_{TLS}(t_{n+m}) - f_{TLS}(t_n) \right]^2, \qquad \tau_m = m\Delta t.$$
 (S.21)

We fit this quantity to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck form

$$MSD(\tau) = \frac{2D}{\gamma} \left( 1 - e^{-\gamma \tau} \right), \qquad \tau_c = \frac{1}{\gamma}, \tag{S.22}$$

## PDF page 18

The nominal analysis yields

$$D = 0.93^{+0.18}_{-0.22} \,\text{MHz}^2 \,\text{s}^{-1}, \qquad \tau_c = 32^{+13}_{-11} \,\text{s},$$
 (S.23)

where the uncertainties are 68% block-bootstrap intervals. Because the MSD values at neighboring lags are correlated, we do not use the covariance matrix of the nonlinear least-squares fit as the uncertainty estimate. Instead, we draw five contiguous blocks of 200 samples ( $\approx 204\,\mathrm{s}$ ) with replacement from the tracked trajectory. The squared displacements entering Eq. (S.21) are evaluated only within each block, avoiding artificial jumps at block boundaries, and the OU model is refitted for each of 1000 bootstrap realizations. The resulting 95% intervals are  $D \in [0.57, 1.28]\,\mathrm{MHz}^2\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$  and  $\tau_c \in [13,61]\,\mathrm{s}$ .

We additionally rerun the complete smoothing, background-subtraction, Viterbi-tracking, and fitting procedure over a  $3 \times 3 \times 3$  robustness grid. The frequency-window width is varied between 0.8, 1.0, and 1.2 times its nominal value; the Gaussian smoothing standard deviation is varied between 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 samples along both map axes; and the Viterbi jump penalty is varied between 5, 10, and 20. Across all 27 combinations, the fitted values remain within

$$D \in [0.70, 0.96] \,\mathrm{MHz^2 \, s^{-1}}, \qquad \tau_c \in [23, 38] \,\mathrm{s.}$$
 (S.24)

When varied one parameter at a time, the corresponding ranges are D=0.76–0.93 MHz $^2$  s $^{-1}$  and  $\tau_c=26$ –32 s for the ROI width, D=0.89–0.94 MHz $^2$  s $^{-1}$  and  $\tau_c=29$ –38 s for smoothing, and D=0.90–0.93 MHz $^2$  s $^{-1}$  and  $\tau_c=32$ –37 s for the Viterbi penalty. Thus, the conclusion that this feature is a rapidly diffusing TLS is insensitive to reasonable analysis choices, while the bootstrap interval captures the larger uncertainty associated with finite-duration sampling of the stochastic trajectory.

### IV. ON- AND OFF-TLS RANDOMIZED BENCHMARKING COMPARISON

Figure [S9](#page-18-1) compares the randomized-benchmarking gate infidelity r<sup>g</sup> to the infidelity expected from energy relaxation for two otherwise similar interleaved measurements. The points are the raw values from individual repetitions, while the solid curves are moving averages. Panel (a) shows the on-TLS experiment of Fig. 3(b) of the main text, while in (b) the same interleaved experiment is performed a few MHz away from the TLS feature of (a). For each repetition, the relaxation-limited contribution is computed as in Ref. [\[6\]](#page-19-7):


$$r_{\Gamma_1}(t_n) = \frac{t_{\text{gate}}}{3T_1(t_n)},\tag{S.25}$$

with tgate = 20 ns. Both traces are plotted as moving averages over a centered time window of width 5 s,

$$\overline{x}(t_n) = \frac{1}{N_n} \sum_{m: |t_m - t_n| \le 2.5 \text{ s}} x(t_m).$$
(S.26)

For the RB trace, the shaded interval is the standard error of the values inside the same moving window. For the relaxation-limited trace, the shaded interval is obtained by propagating the posterior standard deviation of T<sup>1</sup> through Eq. [\(S.25\)](#page-18-2) and then computing the standard error of the windowed mean,

$$\sigma_{\overline{r}_{\Gamma_1}}(t_n) = \frac{1}{N_n} \sqrt{\sum_{m: |t_m - t_n| \le 2.5 \text{ s}} \left[ \frac{t_{\text{gate}}}{3T_1(t_m)^2} \, \sigma_{T_1}(t_m) \right]^2}. \tag{S.27}$$

To quantify whether the measured RB infidelity follows the relaxation-limited trace, we compute the zero-lag normalized cross-correlation. Let x<sup>n</sup> = rg(tn) and y<sup>n</sup> = r<sup>Γ</sup><sup>1</sup> (tn). The correlation is

$$\rho_0 = \frac{\sum_n (x_n - \overline{x}) (y_n - \overline{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_n (x_n - \overline{x})^2 \sum_n (y_n - \overline{y})^2}},$$
(S.28)

Statistical significance is estimated using a circular-shift permutation test: the y<sup>n</sup> trace is randomly rolled by a nonzero number of samples, and the zero-lag correlation is recomputed. The empirical p-value is

$$p = \frac{1 + N_{\text{null}}(|\rho_{\text{null}}| \ge |\rho_{\text{obs}}|)}{1 + N_{\text{perm}}},$$
 (S.29)

with Nperm = 2000.

For the same 5 s moving-averaged traces shown in Fig. [S9,](#page-18-1) the on-TLS dataset has a zero-lag correlation of ρ<sup>0</sup> = 0.618 with p = 5.0 × 10<sup>−</sup><sup>4</sup> . For the off-TLS dataset, the corresponding correlation is ρ<sup>0</sup> = −0.0507 with p = 0.64. Thus, the on-TLS measurement exhibits a strong and statistically significant correlation between the RB infidelity and the relaxation-limited contribution, whereas the off-TLS control is consistent with no correlation. In addition, the off-TLS measurement exhibits both a lower mean relaxation rate and a lower mean RB infidelity than the on-TLS measurement.

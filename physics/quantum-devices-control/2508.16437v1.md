Achieving high-fidelity single-qubit gates, two-qubit gates, and qubit readout is critical for building scalable, error-corrected quantum computers. However, device parameters that enhance one operation often degrade the others, making simultaneous optimization challenging. Here, we demonstrate that careful tuning of qubit–coupler coupling strengths in a superconducting circuit with two transmon qubits coupled via a tunable coupler enables high-fidelity single- and two-qubit gates, without compromising readout performance. As a result, we achieve a 40 h-averaged CZ gate fidelity of 99.93 %, simultaneous single-qubit gate fidelities of 99.98 %, and readout fidelities over 99.94 % in a single device. These results are enabled by optimized coupling parameters, an efficient CZ gate calibration experiment based on our new Phased-Averaged Leakage Error Amplification (PALEA) protocol, and a readout configuration compatible with high coherence qubits. Our results demonstrate a viable path toward scaling up superconducting quantum processors while maintaining consistently high fidelities across all core operations.

# I. INTRODUCTION

Scalable, fault-tolerant quantum computing requires low error rates across all core operations: single-qubit gates, two-qubit gates, and qubit readout. To enable resource-efficient implementation of logical operations, these error rates must fall well below the thresholds set by quantum error correction protocols [\[1–](#page-9-0)[3\]](#page-10-0). Among various quantum hardware platforms, superconducting qubits – particularly transmons – have emerged as a scalable and practical platform for quantum computing, owing to their favorable coherence properties, flexible control, and mature fabrication techniques [\[4–](#page-10-1)[6\]](#page-10-2).

In such systems, single-qubit gate fidelities now routinely exceed 99.9 % [\[3,](#page-10-0) [7](#page-10-3)[–10\]](#page-10-4), and comparable fidelities have been achieved for two-qubit gates [\[11–](#page-10-5)[14\]](#page-10-6). This progress has been driven by recent advances in tunable coupler designs, such as grounded transmon couplers [\[11,](#page-10-5) [15](#page-10-7)[–19\]](#page-10-8), floating transmon couplers [\[20](#page-10-9)[–22\]](#page-10-10), inductively mediated coupling schemes [\[13,](#page-10-11) [14\]](#page-10-6), and doubletransmon couplers [\[12\]](#page-10-12), all of which enable dynamically controlled qubit–qubit interactions while suppressing residual coupling [\[23\]](#page-10-13).

At the same time, high-fidelity readout [\[24–](#page-10-14)[29\]](#page-10-15) has become increasingly critical, not only for final measurements but also for mid-circuit measurements required in quantum error correction [\[3,](#page-10-0) [30,](#page-10-16) [31\]](#page-10-17) and feedbackbased protocols [\[32](#page-10-18)[–34\]](#page-10-19). Advances such as shelving techniques [\[26,](#page-10-20) [35](#page-10-21)[–37\]](#page-10-22) and optimized readout chains incorporating traveling wave parametric amplifiers (TW-PAs [\[38,](#page-10-23) [39\]](#page-11-0) have played a key role in pushing performance forward. Leveraging these developments, recent experiments have reported readout fidelities exceeding 99.9 % [\[28,](#page-10-24) [29\]](#page-10-15).

Despite these advances, simultaneously achieving highfidelity single-qubit gates, two-qubit gates, and readout in a single device remains a major challenge. Device parameters optimized for one operation often degrade another. A key trade-off lies in the qubit–coupler coupling strength: strong coupling facilitates fast entangling gates, but also increases qubit-qubit hybridisation, leading to simultaneous single-qubit gate errors. Similarly, incorporating high-fidelity readout without degrading qubit coherence or gate performance requires careful architectural integration.

In this work, we demonstrate a holistic optimization strategy that addresses the trade-offs between singlequbit gates and two-qubit gates, while maintaining high-

In this qubit–coupler–qubit topology, the couplings between Qubit 1 (Q<sub>1</sub>) and the coupler,  $g_{1c}$ , Qubit 2 (Q<sub>2</sub>) and the coupler,  $g_{2c}$ , as well as the direct qubit-qubit interaction  $g_{12}$  between Q<sub>1</sub> and Q<sub>2</sub> play a crucial role in the performance of both single- and two-qubit gates. For simplicity, we define a symmetric coupling  $g_{qc} := g_{1c} = g_{2c}$  for the numerical analysis.

While tunable couplers are usually employed to cancel the static ZZ interaction between the computational states, the bare qubit states typically remain hybridised for most qubit–qubit detunings in this type of circuit [18, 23, 47], see Appendix B 1. This residual hybridisation gives rise to hybridisation crosstalk – a coherent crosstalk mechanism in which driving one qubit unintentionally excites or induces phase shifts in other unwanted eigenstates, even in the absence of direct coupling between the driveline and those qubits. Consequently, minimizing the transverse coupling strength – governed by  $g_{\rm qc}$  and  $g_{12}$  – helps reduce such unwanted crosstalk, improving simultaneous single-qubit gate fidelities.

However, two-qubit gates benefit from a stronger  $g_{\rm qc}$ . First, higher couplings allow faster CZ gate operation, reducing incoherent error from relaxation and dephasing. Second, with stronger coupling, the same effective qubit-qubit interaction strength can be achieved with a smaller frequency excursion of the coupler. This arrangement reduces the hybridisation of the computational states with the coupler mode during the gate, thereby mitigating the impact of the coupler's typically lower coherence on the gate fidelity [17, 18, 46].

To explore this trade-off quantitatively, we simulate gate errors as a function of qubit-coupler coupling strength  $g_{\rm qc},$  see Fig. 1(b). The simulations assume qubit relaxation times of  $T_1^{\rm Q1} = T_1^{\rm Q2} = 100 \, \mu \text{s}$ , together with qubit and coupler frequencies and anharmonicities being similar to those of our fabricated device (see Appendix A). In the model, both qubits are assumed to have identical  $T_1$  and pure dephasing times  $T_{\phi}$ . The coupler is assigned a relaxation time that is four times shorter than that of the qubits, reflecting its higher energy participation ratio (EPR) in lossy interfaces [48], which – based on our present design – is estimated to be four times larger. All components are modeled as coupled three-level Duffing oscillators [4]. Additionally, to isolate the impact of  $g_{qc}$ , the direct qubit-qubit coupling  $g_{12}$  is adjusted for each value of  $g_{qc}$  such that the coupler idling frequency (where  $\zeta = 0$ ) remains fixed throughout the sweep.

For single-qubit gates, we use master equation simulations to evaluate simultaneous  $\sqrt{X}$  operations on both qubits to capture both incoherent and coherent (hybridisation-induced) errors. We also simulate the CZ gate to extract leakage and incoherence contributions, see Appendix B for further details. The resulting trends show that single-qubit errors increase with  $g_{\rm qc}$ , while CZ gate errors decrease. To evaluate overall gate performance, we calculate the estimated two-qubit Clifford

gate error by weighting the single-qubit gate and twoqubit gate errors by their average prevalence in a random Clifford gate:  $4.65 \sqrt{X}$  gates and 1.5 CZ gates per Clifford [2, 49]. These weighted error contributions yield the Clifford error curve shown in Fig. 1(b).

From this analysis, we identify an optimal coupling strength near  $g_{\rm qc}/(2\pi)=75\,{\rm MHz}$  that minimizes the total Clifford error. The fabricated device yields  $g_{\rm 1c}/(2\pi)=69\,{\rm MHz}$  and  $g_{\rm 2c}/(2\pi)=63\,{\rm MHz}$ , corresponding to a geometric average  $g_{\rm qc}/(2\pi)=66\,{\rm MHz}$ , in close agreement with the design target.

To investigate the role of coherence, we repeat the simulation with  $T_1$  values ranging from 10 µs to 1 ms. As shown in Fig. 1(c), we observe that the optimal  $g_{\rm qc}$  decreases with increasing  $T_1$  due to the hybridisation errors of the single-qubit gates becoming the dominating error mechanism, see Appendix B for further details.

We also compare our results to selected prior works that employ similar transmon-based qubit–coupler–qubit topology and report both  $T_1$  and  $g_{\rm qc}$  [17, 18, 20–22, 46]. Most devices operate within a  $\pm 5\,\%$  range of the simulated optimal coupling. Notably, the device in Ref. [17] stands out as an exception, employing a significantly higher  $g_{\rm qc}$  (by a factor of 3 to 4), possibly due to focusing solely on the CZ gate fidelity. We emphasize, however, that our simulation assumptions do not exactly match those of the compared works, as the qubit and coupler frequencies, anharmonicities, and coherence times differ from our model. Hence, this comparison should be regarded as qualitative.

Our findings provide guidance for future designs: as qubit coherence times increase beyond  $100\,\mu s$  [50, 51], further reducing  $g_{\rm qc}$  becomes beneficial. This not only lowers single-qubit and residual errors but also helps mitigate long-range hybridisation effects such as nextnearest-neighbor crosstalk – an increasingly important factor in larger quantum processing units (QPUs) [22].

### III. CZ GATE CALIBRATION

In this work, the CZ gate is implemented using simultaneous flux pulses on both the coupler and  $Q_1$ , with virtual Z rotations applied to both qubits at the end of the gate to correct for dynamical phases. The flux pulse applied to the qubit consists of a constant plateau between cosine-shaped rising and falling edges to bring the  $|11\rangle$  and  $|02\rangle$  states rapidly close to resonance. The coupler flux pulse follows a Slepian-shaped envelope to minimize population leakage from  $Q_1$  to the coupler during the gate [52]. To account for distortions in the flux lines, we pre-compensate the pulses using exponential filters [53].

Assuming a fixed gate duration, the calibration of this CZ gate implementation involves the minimization of three main imperfections: single-qubit Z rotations, which we offset using virtual Z gates [49]; the ZZ over-rotation angle, which is most sensitive to the frequency of  $Q_1$  during the gate [22]; and the population leakage to  $|02\rangle$ , sen-

As design and coherence time improvements enable higher fidelity gates, the calibration techniques for all the necessary gate parameters need to improve in both precision and speed to keep the gate fidelity limited by incoherent errors. This can be achieved through methods based on phase estimation, employing error amplification with n gates to approach the Heisenberg limit of error O(1/n), such as robust phase estimation [\[54,](#page-11-15) [55\]](#page-11-16), Floquet calibration [\[56\]](#page-11-17), or Matrix-Element Amplification using Dynamical Decoupling (MEADD) [\[57\]](#page-11-18).

Here, we use the MEADD protocol to minimize the error in two of the three CZ gate parameters: the ZZ rotation angle, and the single-qubit virtual Z rotations. Precise optimization of the third imperfection – leakage to the |02⟩, caused by the required diabatic transition between the |11⟩ and |02⟩ states – is more challenging. Most existing error amplification protocols which could be used to minimize the |02⟩ state leakage through extending them to the two-excitation subspace are either prone to interference by other error sources, slow, or incompatible with our hardware, see discussion in Appendix [C.](#page-16-0) To address this, we introduce a modified version of the MEADD protocol, called Phase-Averaged Leakage Error Amplification (PALEA), that employs higher excited state dynamics and phase averaging to isolate the leakage parameter, eliminating the need to control or calibrate the qubit drive phase at each repetition and thereby simplifying and accelerating the implementation in our architecture.

#### A. Leakage Error Amplification

The diabatic CZ gate between transmon qubits with tunable couplers is frequently described in terms of tuning the states |11⟩ and |02⟩ to resonance, and then activating their coupling for a fixed time [\[58\]](#page-11-19). In practice, however, these two levels are not exactly on resonance, and thus this model is often insufficient. Here, we model an imperfect CZ gate as an arbitrary two-level unitary Uˆ <sup>g</sup> ∈ SU(2) acting within the span({|11⟩, |02⟩}) subspace in the laboratory reference frame (see discussion in Appendix [C 1\)](#page-16-1). Ignoring the common phase acquired by both the |11⟩ and |02⟩ states, this unitary can be decomposed as Rˆ <sup>z</sup>(α)Rˆ <sup>x</sup>(θ)Rˆ <sup>z</sup>(β), where Rˆ <sup>j</sup> (ϕ) = exp(−i ϕ 2 σˆ<sup>j</sup> ) denotes a rotation by angle ϕ around axis j, and σ<sup>j</sup> is the corresponding Pauli operator in the {|11⟩, |02⟩} basis [\[59\]](#page-11-20). The resulting action on the |11⟩ and |02⟩ states is thus given by:


$$\begin{split} \hat{U}_g \left| 11 \right\rangle &= e^{i\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}} \cos(\theta/2) \left| 11 \right\rangle + i e^{i\frac{\alpha-\beta}{2}} \sin(\theta/2) \left| 02 \right\rangle \\ \hat{U}_g \left| 02 \right\rangle &= i e^{-i\frac{\alpha-\beta}{2}} \sin(\theta/2) \left| 11 \right\rangle - e^{-i\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}} \cos(\theta/2) \left| 02 \right\rangle \end{split} \tag{1}$$

The angle of interest, θ, governs the population transfer between the |11⟩ and |02⟩ states and is primarily controlled by the coupler frequency – and thus by the coupler flux pulse amplitude. Any deviation from θ = 0 indicates an error. Hence, the aim of a leakage amplification experiment is to precisely measure the over-rotation angle θ. In contrast, the angles α and β, describing the difference of phases acquired by states |11⟩ and |02⟩, are irrelevant, as the |02⟩ state lies outside the computational basis.

The basic leakage amplification experiment [\[18\]](#page-10-25) repeatedly applies CZ gate candidates to an initial |11⟩ state, see Fig. [2\(](#page-3-0)a). As shown in Fig. [2\(](#page-3-0)b), sweeping both the number of CZ gates and the coupler flux pulse amplitude while measuring the population of the |02⟩ state (noted as unwanted population in Fig. [2\(](#page-3-0)b)) reveals low contrast

# B. PALEA protocol

To avoid the aforementioned issues in amplifying the over-rotation angle  $\theta$ , we introduce the PALEA protocol. A dynamical decoupling (DD) layer D is added after each CZ gate, consisting of an  $X_{\pi}^{12}(\phi_1)$  gate with phase  $\phi_1$ , acting on the span( $\{|1\rangle, |2\rangle\}$ ) subspace of the higher frequency transmon and a phased- $X_{\pi}^{01}(\phi_0)$  gate with phase  $\phi_0$  on the lower frequency transmon, see Fig. 2(c). Hence, D acts on the span( $\{|11\rangle, |02\rangle\}$ ) subspace as a  $\pi$  rotation:


$$D = \hat{R}_z(\phi_0 - \phi_1)\hat{R}_x(\pi)\hat{R}_z(\phi_1 - \phi_0). \tag{2}$$

This is essentially an adaptation of the SWAP error protocol from Ref. [57] (Eqs. 55-60), adjusting the DD layer to act on the span( $\{|11\rangle, |02\rangle\}$ ) subspace instead of the span( $\{|10\rangle, |01\rangle\}$ ) subspace. Importantly, to correctly implement the D operation in the laboratory frame, special care needs to be taken to ensure that the phases of the subsequent D layers within the same gate sequence are properly accounted for, see Appendix D 2.

After n cycles of CZ gates followed by the DD layers the probability of measuring  $|11\rangle$  is:


$$V_n(\theta, \Delta \phi) = \left| \langle 11 | \left[ \hat{R}_z(\Delta \phi) \hat{R}_x(\pi - \theta) \right]^n | 11 \rangle \right|^2, \quad (3)$$

where  $\Delta \phi = 2\phi_0 - 2\phi_1 - \alpha + \beta$  depends both on the difference of the phases of the applied  $X_{\pi}^{01}$  and  $X_{\pi}^{12}$ gates and the intrinsic Z rotations induced by each  $\stackrel{\circ}{\text{CZ}}$ gate between the  $|11\rangle$  and  $|02\rangle$  states, see discussion in Appendix D. Due to the D layer acting as a  $\pi$  rotation between the states  $|11\rangle$  and  $|02\rangle$ , the cycle unitary  $\hat{R}_{z}(\Delta\phi)\hat{R}_{x}(\pi-\theta)$  is now dominated by the X rotation rather than the Z rotation for small  $\theta$  values, greatly increasing visibility in the final Z measurement. In contrast to the MEADD protocol, which evaluates the dynamics for only two specific values of  $\Delta \phi$ , PALEA averages over a wide range of random angles. This averaging is introduced either passively – through the inability to reset the phase difference between the  $X_\pi^{01}$  and  $X_\pi^{12}$  gates between repetitions, as is common for systems using CZbased gate sets – or it can be implemented actively by manually varying the phase difference in each repetition.

When sampling a broad range of  $\Delta \phi$  values the expected value of the measured  $|11\rangle$  state population is given by


$$I_n(\pi - \theta) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} d\phi \ V_n(\theta, \phi). \tag{4}$$

The purpose of the averaging is to decouple the unimportant angles  $\alpha$  and  $\beta$  from the target over-rotation angle  $\theta$  and perform only one averaged measurement.

The integral in Eq. (4) can be solved analytically (see Appendix D 3), yielding a closed-form expression for the signal in the PALEA experiment:


$$I_n(\pi - \theta) = 1 - \cos^2(\theta/2) \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} (-1)^m P_m(\cos \theta),$$
 (5)

where  $P_m$  is the m-th Legendre polynomial. The model predicts a high-contrast signal without requiring additional calibration – adaptive or otherwise – of any other parameter, allowing for an accurate estimation of  $\theta$  with fewer measurement repetitions. Eq. (5) can be quickly evaluated using the Clenshaw recursion algorithm for Legendre series, see Appendix D 4.

#### C. Calibration using PALEA

By fitting the model to experimental data, we can accurately fit the over-rotation angle  $\theta$ , and hence, the leakage to the  $|02\rangle$  state. For example, the signal  $I_n$  at a coupler flux pulse amplitude of 0.436 [Fig. 2(d)] can be fitted with the model in Eq. (5) to extract  $|\theta_{\rm fit}| = 0.383 \pm 0.002$ , corresponding to a population transfer to the  $|02\rangle$  state of  $\sin^2(\theta_{\rm fit}/2) \approx 3.6\%$  per CZ gate, see Fig. 2(e). Conversely, the standard error amplification experiment yields a low-contrast signal for the coupler flux pulse amplitude of 0.436 [Fig. 2(b)], oscillating at a faster frequency  $2\mu = 2\cos^{-1}(\cos(\theta/2)\cos[(\alpha + \beta)/2])$  (Appendix C).

As shown in Fig. 2(f), averaging the PALEA signal over the number of cycles yields a sharp dip which can be accurately fitted with a simple Lorentzian model to determine the optimal coupler flux pulse amplitude  $x_0$ . This enables fast and efficient calibration of a high fidelity CZ gate. In contrast, averaging the signal of the standard error amplification experiment produces a broader dip, reducing the precision of identifying the optimal coupler flux pulse amplitude and potentially increasing the leakage to the  $|02\rangle$  state – unless a significantly higher number of repetitions is used, see Appendix D 4.

# IV. OPTIMIZING AND BENCHMARKING THE CZ GATE

To accurately characterize gate fidelity, we use iterative interleaved randomized benchmarking (IRB) [13, 22, 63].

In this protocol, n CZ gates are interleaved between Clifford gates instead of just one. Fig. 3(a) illustrates the protocol for n = 1, 3, 5. The extracted total error after applying n interleaved CZ gates,  $\epsilon(n)$ , is shown in Fig. 3(b). We fit this dependence to a quadratic model,

$$\epsilon_{\text{quad}}(n) = an^2 + bn + c$$

to disentangle coherent and incoherent error sources: incoherent errors increase linearly with n, while coherent errors and errors with long temporal correlations accumulate quadratically [63]. A further advantage of iterative IRB is the explicit extraction of the constant offset term c, which may arise from residual non-Markovian effects – such as flux or drive pulse distortions from the surrounding Clifford gates [9] or coherent flux noise [64] – unrelated to the interleaved CZ gates. In standard IRB, this offset is indistinguishable from the CZ gate error, leading to systematic over- or underestimation, whereas iterative IRB separates it from the intrinsic gate error.

We define the CZ gate error as the slope of the curve at n=1, i.e.,  $\epsilon_{\rm CZ}:=\epsilon'_{\rm quad}(1)=2a+b$  [65, 66]. Unlike  $\epsilon_{\rm quad}(1)$ , which resembles the gate error definition of standard IRB,  $\epsilon'_{\rm quad}(1)$  is independent of c while still capturing the coherent error of a single CZ gate. Although coherent error contribution grows with n, in many applications such residual errors can be converted into incoherent errors using protocols such as randomized compiling [67], ensuring that the coherent errors do not add up constructively.

To minimize coherent CZ gate errors, we calibrate both the coupler and qubit flux pulse amplitudes using PALEA, discussed in Section III. In addition, we optimize the gate durations by sweeping the coupler and qubit flux pulse widths separately. Full re-calibration and iterative IRB with three-state discrimination is performed for each duration setting to benchmark both the CZ gate error and the leakage to the  $|2\rangle$  states (see Appendix G). We find the optimal CZ gate performance at a total gate duration of 33 ns, consisting of a 22 ns coupler pulse overlapping with a 27 ns qubit pulse and 3 ns buffers on each side.

After calibration and optimization of the CZ gate, we benchmark it using iterative IRB over a continuous period of 40 h, re-calibrating only the single-qubit gates between measurements [Fig. 3(c)]. For each run of the iterative IRB experiment, we use a fixed seed to generate the set of Clifford sequences, and find an average CZ error of

$$\bar{\epsilon}_{\rm CZ} = (6.5 \pm 0.2) \times 10^{-4},$$

where the uncertainty is the standard error of the mean. The lowest observed CZ gate error during the measurement series was  $\epsilon_{\rm CZ}^{\rm min} = (4.9 \pm 0.7) \times 10^{-4}$ . The histogram of data points in Fig. 3(d) confirms a nearly Gaussian distribution with standard deviation  $\sigma(\bar{\epsilon}_{\rm CZ}) = 1.1 \times 10^{-4}$ .

The corresponding average Clifford gate error is measured to be

$$\bar{\epsilon}_{\text{Clifford}} = (2.79 \pm 0.05) \times 10^{-3},$$

in good agreement with the estimate based on the Clifford gate decomposition

$$\epsilon_{\text{Clifford}}^{\text{estimate}} = 1 - \left( (1 - \epsilon_{\text{CZ}})^{1.5} \cdot (1 - \epsilon_{\sqrt{X}})^{4.65} \right) = 2.4 \times 10^{-3},$$

using  $\epsilon_{\sqrt{X}} \approx 3 \times 10^{-4}$ . Note that in these experiments, the single-qubit gate duration was set to 40 ns, which had not yet been fully optimized. The improved fidelities presented later in the paper use shorter, optimized gate durations.

We also compare the iterative IRB result to the CZ error estimated using standard IRB analysis, which yields

$$\bar{\epsilon}_{CZ}^{IRB} = (7.3 \pm 0.2) \times 10^{-4}$$
.

This value is significantly higher than the gate error extracted from iterative IRB. In our data, the quadratic fit reveals an average offset of  $c=1.1\times 10^{-4}$ , likely from residual non-Markovian effects unrelated to the CZ gate. Subtracting this offset from the standard IRB result yields  $\bar{\epsilon}_{\rm CZ}^{\rm IRB} - c = 6.2\times 10^{-4}$ , in close agreement with the iterative IRB value, indicating that standard IRB mistakenly attributes the offset c to the CZ gate error.

#### V. ERROR BUDGET OF THE FULL SYSTEM

To evaluate the overall performance of our quantum device, we construct a comprehensive error budget encompassing all core operations: single-qubit gates, two-qubit (CZ) gates, and readout. While the CZ gate fidelity is a key benchmark for entangling operations, achieving consistently low errors across all layers of the control stack is critical for scalability and fault-tolerant performance. This section details how each component is individually optimized, calibrated, and characterized.

#### A. Single-qubit gates

Single-qubit gates are implemented using cosine-shaped envelopes combined with the derivative removal by adiabatic gate (DRAG) technique [68, 69] to suppress leakage to the second excited state. The quadrature envelope is optimized for leakage minimization, and the resulting phase errors are corrected using virtual Z gates [49]. Our native gate set consists of  $\sqrt{X}$  rotations.

To quantify the single-qubit gate fidelity, we use purity randomized benchmarking (RB) with three-state discrimination to capture incoherent, coherent, and leakage errors [9, 70–72]. We sweep the gate duration to identify the optimal operating point, finding a minimum error at 26 ns. This relatively long duration, compared to recent literature [9, 73], is primarily constrained by compression effects in the up-conversion mixer, which limit the usable drive power.

Figures 4(a)–(b) show the purity RB results for Q<sub>1</sub>, categorizing single-qubit gate errors into incoherent, coherent, and leakage errors. We extract a single-qubit

gate error of  $\epsilon_{\sqrt{X}}^{\mathrm{Q1}}=(1.59\pm0.02)\times10^{-4}$ , with the dominant contribution from incoherent error,  $\epsilon_{\sqrt{X}}^{\mathrm{Q1,incoh}}=(1.26\pm0.01)\times10^{-4}$ . The residual error consists of coherent calibration errors such as control pulse distortion, and a negligible amount of leakage errors to the  $|2\rangle$ , remaining below the measurable threshold of  $1\times10^{-6}$ . Figures 4(c)–(d) show analogous results for  $Q_2$ , where we find  $\epsilon_{\sqrt{X}}^{\mathrm{Q2}}=(1.68\pm0.02)\times10^{-4}$ , composed of  $\epsilon_{\sqrt{X}}^{\mathrm{Q2,incoh}}=(1.62\pm0.01)\times10^{-4}$ ,  $\epsilon_{\sqrt{X}}^{\mathrm{Q2,coh}}=(4\pm2)\times10^{-6}$ , and leakage  $\epsilon_{\sqrt{X}}^{\mathrm{Q2,leak}}=(3.7\pm0.5)\times10^{-6}$ .

The low leakage errors are expected given the relatively long gate durations used and the additional suppression from the DRAG pulse. Simulations for a  $\sqrt{X}$  gate with a duration of 26 ns, qubit-qubit detuning of 110 MHz, and qubit relaxation times of  $T_1 \approx 100 \,\mu s$  predict a gate error of  $\epsilon_{\sqrt{X}}^{\text{sim}} = 1.3 \times 10^{-4}$  for both qubits, fully limited by incoherent error, agreeing well with the measured values. At these gate durations, detuning, and optimized qubit-coupler couplings, the expected hybridisation error is below  $1 \times 10^{-5}$ , and therefore does not limit the performance. This is experimentally confirmed by comparing gate fidelities of the two qubits individually, where we observe nearly identical performance indicating negligible hybridisation error. These results also imply that the microwave crosstalk, measured to be approximately  $-20 \,\mathrm{dB}$ , is not a limiting factor in our setup at the given qubit-qubit detuning.

We note that the simulated  $\sqrt{X}$  gate errors in Fig. 1(b) are based on optimally short gate durations (on the order of 15 ns), while our experiments use longer pulses due to limitations mentioned above. Hence, the simulated gate error reaches  $1 \times 10^{-4}$ , even when averaging over non-ideal qubit-qubit detunings.

### B. CZ gate error decomposition

To assess the two-qubit gate performance, we use the iterative IRB method described in Section IV. In addition to measuring the CZ gate error directly, we seek to disentangle its error contributions.

We first determine the coherence-limited error floor of the qubits by applying iterative IRB with an interleaved identity (ID) gate of the same duration as the CZ gate (33 ns). The extracted error per ID gate is  $\epsilon_{\rm ID}=(4.1\pm0.2)\times10^{-4}$  [Fig. 4(e)], which serves as an estimate for the lower bound of CZ gate error in the absence of coupler-induced decoherence,  $\epsilon_{\rm CZ}^{\rm lim}$ .

Next, we estimate the incoherent component of the CZ gate error,  $\epsilon_{\rm CZ}^{\rm incoh}$ , by measuring  $T_1$  and  $T_2$  of the hybridised qubit modes as a function of the coupler flux bias, and integrating over the flux pulse waveform to compute effective coherence times (see Appendix H). This yields  $\epsilon_{\rm CZ}^{\rm incoh} = 6.2 \times 10^{-4}$ . To characterize leakage errors to higher qubit excited states, we implement leakage randomized benchmarking (LRB) [72, 74] using three-state discrimination of the iterative IRB data. We extract a total leakage error to the second excited states of both qubits,  $L_{\rm CZ}^{\rm tot}=1\times 10^{-4}$ . Most leakage originates from Q<sub>2</sub> ( $L_{\rm CZ}^{\rm Q2}=9\times 10^{-5}$ ), which transiently populates the  $|2\rangle$  state during the CZ gate.

Summing the incoherent and leakage contributions gives  $\epsilon_{\rm CZ}^{\rm incoh} + L_{\rm CZ}^{\rm tot} = 7.2 \times 10^{-4}$ , which closely matches the measured average CZ error of  $\bar{\epsilon}_{\rm CZ} = 6.5 \times 10^{-4}$ , suggesting that most error contributions are captured by this decomposition. The residual error is attributed to coherent error  $\epsilon_{\rm CZ}^{\rm coh}$ , which manifests as a quadratic contribution in iterative IRB and is likely caused by flux pulse distortions that induce inter-gate memory effects.

We also characterize the SWAP and coupler leakage errors and demonstrate that neither limits the performance of our CZ gate. The SWAP error arises from coherent population exchange between the  $|10\rangle$  and  $|01\rangle$  states and is described by a two-level unitary operator  $\hat{U}_s$  acting in the span( $\{|10\rangle, |01\rangle\}$ ) subspace. Analogous

to the leakage analysis in Section III,  $\hat{U}_s$  is decomposed as  $\hat{R}_z(\alpha_s)\hat{R}_x(\theta_s)\hat{R}_z(\beta_s)$ . Here,  $\theta_s$  quantifies the SWAP error, while  $\alpha_s$  and  $\beta_s$  correspond to conditional and single-qubit phases that can be calibrated separately. Using PALEA, we measure  $\theta_s = (0.0198 \pm 0.0006) \, \mathrm{rad}$ , which corresponds to a SWAP error of  $\epsilon_{\mathrm{CZ}}^{\mathrm{SWAP}} = (3.9 \pm 0.2) \times 10^{-5}$ , confirming that SWAP errors are not a limiting factor for the CZ gate fidelity (see Appendix F 1 for further discussion).

Leakage to the coupler is more challenging to quantify, as the coupler lacks direct XY control and readout. The absence of a dedicated drive and measurement line precludes the use of PALEA or randomized benchmarking techniques to assess population transfer to the coupler excited state. Instead, we adopt a modified Floquet calibration method [56], in which a variable delay  $\tau$  is inserted between repeated CZ gates. During this delay, the state  $|101\rangle$  and the leaked state  $|011\rangle$  acquire a relative phase under the free Hamiltonian evolution of the system. For specific  $\tau$  values, this phase evolution constructively amplifies any residual coupler population, enabling its detection. We measure a leakage-

# C. Shelved readout and fidelity analysis

For high-fidelity measurement, we use a shelving-based readout technique: an  $X_\pi^{12}$  gate is applied just prior to a rectangular readout pulse to transfer the  $|e\rangle$  state to  $|f\rangle$ , thereby slightly increasing its effective readout lifetime [26, 35–37]. We begin by optimizing the readout frequency, followed by a joint optimization of the readout amplitude and duration for maximum readout fidelity. Finally, after optimizing the integration weights, the configuration consists of a 40 ns shelving gate followed by a 240 ns readout pulse, resulting in a total duration of 280 ns.

Figures 4(f)–(g) show single-shot in-phase and quadrature distributions for Q<sub>1</sub> and Q<sub>2</sub> based on two million samples.  $|g\rangle$  state preparations are mostly classified as  $|0\rangle$ , with rare  $|\bar{0}\rangle$  misclassifications mostly due to measurement-induced excitations.  $|e\rangle$  state preparations – shelved to  $|f\rangle$  with occasional  $f\to e$  relaxation – are classified as  $|\bar{0}\rangle$ ; rare  $e\to g$  relaxation yields  $|0\rangle$  misclassifications. Counting the assignment errors, we extract readout errors of  $\epsilon_{\rm shelved}^{\rm Q1}=(5.7\pm0.2)\times10^{-4}$  and  $\epsilon_{\rm shelved}^{\rm Q2}=(5.4\pm0.2)\times10^{-4}$ , with uncertainties obtained via bootstrapping [75].

From the histogram fits shown in Figs. 4(h)–(i), we identify the main readout error sources for  $Q_1$  as decay error  $\epsilon_{\rm shelved}^{\rm Q1,decay}=(3.8\pm0.2)\times10^{-4}$ , mixing error  $\epsilon_{\rm shelved}^{\rm Q1,mix}=(1.48\pm0.01)\times10^{-4}$ , and negligible overlap error  $\epsilon_{\rm shelved}^{\rm Q1,overlap}=(3.6\pm0.1)\times10^{-5}$ . For  $Q_2$ , the error profile follows the same pattern, with decay error  $\epsilon_{\rm shelved}^{\rm Q2,decay}=(3.6\pm0.2)\times10^{-4}$  as the leading contribution, while mixing and overlap errors remain at levels comparable to those of  $Q_1$ .

Although shelving increases the effective relaxation time during measurement, qubit decay remains the dominant limitation to the readout fidelity. Potential improvements include shelving to the third excited state, which further extends the effective lifetime of the readout manifold.

While shelving provides high-fidelity readout suitable for final measurements, it is less practical for mid-circuit measurements required in quantum error correction [3] due to the potentially increased risk of leakage to the  $|f\rangle$  state. Compatibility with such measurements can be restored if high-fidelity reset from the  $|f\rangle$  state is avail-

able [76–80], enabling the qubit to be returned to its pre-measurement state when needed.

Ideally, mid-circuit measurements are implemented as quantum non-demolition (QND) readouts, which preserve the qubit state regardless of the measurement outcome [42]. In practice, no readout is perfectly QND, and the goal is to maximize its QNDness Q, defined as [43]:

$$Q = (Q_{g} + Q_{e})/2, \tag{6}$$

with

$$Q_{g} = P(g, 0|g) + P(g, 1|g)$$

$$Q_{e} = P(e, 0|e) + P(e, 1|e),$$
(7)

where P(a, b|c) denotes the probability that the qubit ends up in state a and is assigned outcome b, given the qubit is initially in state c. We adapt the readout-induced leakage benchmarking (RILB) protocol [43] to extract Q; see Appendix I for details on the experiment and models.

Using the fidelity-optimized shelved readout parameters, and appending an additional  $X_{\pi}^{12}$  gate after the readout pulse to return population from  $|f\rangle$  to  $|e\rangle$ , we observe that the QNDness is limited by elevated leakage rates (Table I). This leakage is attributed to the decay from  $|f\rangle$  to  $|e\rangle$  during the readout pulse, followed by reexcitation to  $|f\rangle$  via the second  $X_{\pi}^{12}$  gate.

As an alternative, we optimize a standard (unshelved) readout pulse for QNDness, which typically involves lowering the readout amplitude to reduce the number of photons in the resonator and minimize excitation to higher states [43, 81, 82]. The QND-optimized readout reduces leakage by up to two orders of magnitude relative to the shelved readout and improves the QNDness up to Q = 99.41%, albeit with reduced fidelity, see Table I.

These results place our QND performance alongside other high-fidelity QND measurements reported for QPU-compatible architectures [29, 77, 83–86].

# D. Total system-level error budget

Combining all contributions, we define the total system-level error as

$$\epsilon_{\rm tot} = \sum_{i=1,2} \left( \epsilon_{\sqrt{X}}^{\rm Q} + \epsilon_{\rm RO}^{\rm Q} \right) + \epsilon_{\rm CZ} \approx 2.1 \times 10^{-3}.$$

Depending on the application, different weights may be assigned to each component. For instance, quantum error correction demands low leakage and coherent errors to ensure decoder compatibility [3], while dynamical decoupling schemes would utilize more single-qubit gates, hence increasing their weight. Note that our simple definition of the system error,  $\epsilon_{\rm tot}$ , does not account for all error mechanisms – such as idling errors – which are relevant for quantum error correction.

# VI. CONCLUSIONS

We have demonstrated simultaneous high-fidelity single-qubit gates, CZ gates, and readout in a superconducting quantum device composed of two transmon qubits coupled via a tunable floating coupler. Through targeted device-level simulations, we identified the optimal qubit–coupler coupling strength that minimizes the total Clifford error by balancing hybridisation-induced crosstalk and incoherent gate errors. This design strategy was validated experimentally, yielding single-qubit gate fidelities above 99.98 %, a 40-hour-averaged CZ gate fidelity of 99.93 %, and readout fidelities exceeding 99.94 %.

A key enabler of this performance is our newly developed PALEA (Phase-Averaged Leakage Error Amplification) protocol, which allows precise calibration of coherent over- and under-rotation errors in the span({|11⟩, |02⟩}) subspace. Combined with iterative interleaved randomized benchmarking, PALEA enables both the suppression and quantification of coherent errors, leading to a factor of two reduction in leakage and an accurate estimation of the residual CZ gate error.

We also presented a detailed error budget that quantifies the incoherent, coherent, and leakage components of both single- and two-qubit gates, as well as readout. The readout fidelity was achieved via shelving to the second excited state and was broken down into decay, mixing, and overlap contributions. In addition, we optimized non-shelved readout for QNDness, obtaining values exceeding 99.3 % for both qubits. The total system-level error is estimated to be 2.1 × 10<sup>−</sup><sup>3</sup> , approaching operation errors required to efficiently scale up quantum error correction codes and thus representing a key advance towards scalable, fault-tolerant quantum computing.

Importantly, we demonstrated a device architecture and control methodology compatible with scalable layouts. Our design allows each qubit to be coupled to multiple tunable couplers while preserving gate parameters, and the chip layout supports dedicated Purcell filters for each qubit on a single fabrication layer. These features make our approach well-suited for extension to 2D square-lattice architectures required, for example, for surface code implementations.

Together, our results provide a blueprint for achieving uniformly high fidelities across all core quantum operations in a scalable superconducting platform, bringing fault-tolerant quantum computing closer to reality.

#### APPENDIX A: EXPERIMENTAL SETUP

The experiments are performed in a cryogenic dilution refrigerator with a base temperature of approximately 10 mK. A schematic of the full wiring configuration is shown in Fig. S1. The sample consists of two frequency-tunable floating transmon qubits coupled via a frequency-tunable floating transmon coupler. A summary of the key device parameters is provided in Table S1.

Each qubit has an individual microwave drive line, equipped with  $39\,\mathrm{dB}$  of attenuation distributed across the various temperature stages to minimize thermal noise. Additional low-pass and Eccosorb filters are installed at the mixing chamber stage to suppress spurious high-frequency signals. The Eccosorb filters add approximately another  $15\,\mathrm{dB}$  attenuation at the qubit frequencies.

The qubits are driven using a Zurich Instruments HDAWG arbitrary waveform generator (AWG), which produces intermediate-frequency pulses that are upconverted to the qubit frequencies using IQ mixers. Dedicated HDAWG channels are also used to control the voltage on the flux line of  $Q_1$  and the coupler. For  $Q_2$ , which requires only static flux control, we use a Qblox SPI D5a voltage source module to deliver DC biases.

The readout bus consists of a transmission line capacitively coupled to the readout structure of each qubit. Each readout structure comprises a dedicated resonator—Purcell filter pair, designed to suppress spontaneous emission into the environment while maintaining the coupling strength required for fast, high-fidelity measurement. Readout pulses are generated using a Zurich

Instruments UHFQA, with frequency multiplexing applied to address both qubits simultaneously. The UHFQA output is upconverted and sent through the dilution refrigerator input chain.

The readout signal is first amplified by a three-wave—mixing traveling-wave parametric amplifier (TWPA) at the 10 mK stage and subsequently by a 4 K high-electron-mobility transistor (HEMT) amplifier. The amplified signal is then downconverted at room temperature and digitized by the UHFQA. The TWPA is pumped by a Rhode & Schwarz microwave generator at 11 GHz and biased using two DC output channels of a Qblox SPI D5a voltage source.

For reference, Fig. S1 includes the location and labeling of all attenuators, filters, amplifiers, and circulators, as well as the thermal stage at which each component is mounted. All flux and drive lines are thermally anchored at each temperature stage to minimize heat load and thermal noise coupling to the qubits.

#### 3. Single-Qubit Gate Error Mechanisms

Single-qubit gate errors are primarily influenced by incoherent processes, hybridisation-induced crosstalk, and, for short pulses, leakage to higher energy levels. Hybridisation crosstalk arises from the fact that the qubits are slightly hybridised with each other due to the choice of the idling configuration. Although the coupler is biased to cancel the ZZ interaction ( $\zeta=0$ ), the eigenstates of the coupled system  $-|00\rangle$ ,  $|01\rangle$ ,  $|10\rangle$ ,  $|11\rangle$  – are not perfectly aligned with the bare states  $|gg\rangle$ ,  $|ge\rangle$ ,  $|eg\rangle$ ,  $|ee\rangle$  [18]. As a result, driving one qubit can unintentionally excite the hybridised mode of the other, even in the absence of classical crosstalk.

This hybridisation crosstalk depends on (i) the qubit-coupler coupling strength  $g_{\rm qc}$  (larger  $g_{\rm qc}$  leads to stronger hybridisation), (ii) the qubit-qubit detuning  $\Delta \omega$ , and (iii) the gate duration (shorter pulses have broader frequency content and are more likely to excite neighboring eigenstates). To capture these effects, we perform a four-dimensional sweep over  $\sqrt{X}$  gate duration,  $g_{\rm qc}$ , relaxation time  $T_1$  (which sets both  $T_1$  and  $T_{\phi}$ ), and qubit-qubit detuning  $\Delta \omega$ , simulating both qubits simultaneously. The drive waveform is a cosine-shaped envelope, matching our experimental implementation.

# APPENDIX C: DYNAMICS OF THE REPEATED DIABATIC CZ GATE

In this section, we elaborate on the theory behind the standard error amplification experiment [Fig. 2(a) and (b)], as well as its extensions for characterizing leakage to the  $|02\rangle$  state and the coupler. Firstly, the appropriate frame of reference should be chosen.

# 1. Choice of the frame of reference

In this and Sections D and E, we primarily work in the laboratory reference frame, unless specified otherwise. This choice is motivated by the nature of the diabatic CZ gate, which is implemented by dynamically tuning the frequencies of the coupler and qubit  $Q_1$  [22]. As a result, in the laboratory frame, each of the repeated CZ gates in the same gate sequence can be described by the same unitary  $\hat{U}_g$  as in Eq. (1) of the main text. Moving to a standard computational frame, defined by the frequencies of the two qubits, would cause the underlying Hamiltonian

## PDF page 18

$\hat{H}$  to acquire additional time dependence as a function of the difference of the qubit frequencies. Consequently, each consecutive CZ in the same sequence would be described by a different unitary in the span( $\{|11\rangle, |02\rangle\}$ ) subspace. Thus, choosing to instead work in the laboratory frame (or any interaction frame associated with the same frequency  $\omega$  for both qubits) greatly simplifies the derivations.

This choice has minor implementation consequences which will be described later in Appendix D 2.

#### 2. Standard error amplification

The goal of this section is to analyze the dynamics of the standard error amplification experiment shown in Fig. 2(a) and (b) of the main text. In particular, we seek to understand why the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the population oscillations becomes small near the optimal coupler flux pulse amplitude – rendering this method inefficient for minimizing leakage.

To this end, we calculate the expectation value  $V_n$  after applying n repeated CZ gates to the initial state  $|11\rangle$ . We start from the unitary  $\hat{U}_g$  defined in Eq. (1) in the main text, acting on the span( $\{|11\rangle, |02\rangle\}$ ) subspace and decomposed as  $\hat{U}_g = \hat{R}_z(\alpha)\hat{R}_x(\theta)\hat{R}_z(\beta)$ , where  $\theta$  governs the population transfer between  $|11\rangle$  and  $|02\rangle$ . The expectation value for the population remaining in  $|11\rangle$  after n applications of  $\hat{U}_g$  is then:


$$V_n(\theta, \alpha, \beta) = \left| \langle 11 | \left[ \hat{U}_g(\theta, \alpha, \beta) \right]^n | 11 \rangle \right|^2$$

$$= \left| \langle 11 | \left[ \hat{R}_z(\alpha + \beta) \hat{R}_y(\theta) \right]^n | 11 \rangle \right|^2.$$
(S4)

In the second line, we use the identity


$$\left(\hat{R}_z(\alpha)\hat{R}_x(\theta)\hat{R}_z(\beta)\right)^n = \hat{R}_z(\alpha - \frac{\pi}{2})\left(\hat{R}_z(\alpha + \beta)\hat{R}_y(\theta)\right)^n\hat{R}_z(\beta + \frac{\pi}{2}),$$
 (S5)

and note that the leading and trailing Z rotations do not affect the expectation value in a Z basis measurement. We define  $\phi = \alpha + \beta$  as the effective Z rotation applied in each cycle. Since  $\alpha$  and  $\beta$  are not relevant for the operation of a CZ gate,  $\phi$  remains uncalibrated and effectively random, but constant throughout the experiment.

The single cycle operator  $R(\phi,\theta)=R_z(\phi)R_y(\theta)$  can be diagonalized as

$$\hat{R}(\phi, \theta) = S(\phi, \theta)D(\phi, \theta)S^{-1}(\phi, \theta), \tag{S6}$$

where

$$D(\phi, \theta) = \begin{pmatrix} e^{i\mu} & 0\\ 0 & e^{-i\mu} \end{pmatrix}$$
 (S7)

$$S(\phi, \theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \sin(\theta/2) & \sin(\theta/2) \\ \cos(\theta/2) - e^{i(\frac{\phi}{2} + \mu)} & \cos(\theta/2) - e^{i(\frac{\phi}{2} - \mu)} \end{pmatrix}.$$
(S8)

The matrices  $D(\phi, \theta)$  and  $S(\phi, \theta)$  are defined in the  $\{|11\rangle, |02\rangle\}$  basis, and the angle  $\mu$  satisfies


$$\cos \mu = \cos(\phi/2)\cos(\theta/2). \tag{S9}$$

Substituting into Eq. (S4), the expectation value becomes:


$$\left| \langle 11|\hat{R}^n(\phi,\theta)|11\rangle \right|^2 = \left| \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \cdot S(\phi,\theta) D^n(\phi,\theta) S^{-1}(\phi,\theta) \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \right|^2$$
$$= \frac{\cos^2(\theta/2) - \cos(2\mu) + \sin^2(\theta/2) \cos(2n\mu)}{2\sin^2 \mu},$$
(S10)

This expression can be written as  $A+B\cos(2n\mu)$ , where A and B are independent of n. The signal therefore consists of an oscillation with frequency  $2\mu$  and amplitude B, which determines the SNR of the coherent error amplification experiment.

We can express the amplitude B as:


$$2B = \frac{1}{1 + \left(\frac{\sin\frac{\phi}{2}}{\tan\frac{\theta}{2}}\right)^2}.$$
 (S11)

This is a Lorentzian function centered around  $\phi = 0$ , with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of  $2\tan(\theta/2)$ . For arbitrary  $\phi$  and small  $\theta$ , the amplitude B becomes small, leading to poor SNR and ineffective error amplification. This explains the low contrast observed near the optimal coupler flux pulse amplitude (where  $\theta$  is small) in Fig. 2(b) of the main text.

Finally, we note that the signal oscillates at the frequency  $2\mu$  per gate, which satisfies  $\theta \leq 2\mu \leq \pi$ . Eq. (S9) shows that  $2\mu = \theta$  is satisfied for  $\phi = \alpha + \beta = 0$ , but in general  $\mu$  depends on the uncontrolled angle  $\phi$ . This means that the frequency of the measured signal is systematically larger than  $\theta$ , making it difficult to accurately extract the leakage angle based on the oscillation frequency alone, even if the lowered SNR is overcome by increasing the number of repetitions in the experiment.

### 3. Extensions to the standard error amplification

For completeness, in this section we present two known extensions to the standard error amplification experiment that enable accurate measurement of leakage to the  $|02\rangle$  state. A key requirement for these methods is that the CZ gate itself remains unmodified during error amplification. Both extensions aim to compensate for a nonzero angle  $\phi=\alpha+\beta,$  which arises due to the levels  $|11\rangle$  and  $|02\rangle$  being non-resonant during the final calibrated gate and the additional free evolution during the added buffer time. If  $\phi$  is effectively canceled – such that  $\phi\ll\theta$  – then the signal amplitude in Eq. (S11) becomes large, allowing reliable amplification of leakage error.

However, these extensions are not practical for fine calibration tasks. Such tuning is time-consuming and sensitive to fluctuations, even if performed adaptively. Moreover, varying gate parameters in an optimization routine would necessitate adaptation of these compensating operations at each point, limiting their usefulness.

### APPENDIX D: PALEA MODEL

In this section, we present the theoretical framework underlying PALEA and demonstrate its specific application to measuring the leakage from  $|11\rangle$  to  $|02\rangle$ . Finally, we compare the accuracy and precision of PALEA to the other error amplification protocols discussed in the previous sections. In Appendix E 2 we show that PALEA is insensitive to other leakage processes, enabling accurate calibration of a single exchange process at a time.

Similarly to the MEADD protocol [57], PALEA can be generally applied to any excitation-preserving entangling gate as long as small off-resonant exchange rate between the two levels can be assumed. The only two required components are the ability to implement the dynamical decoupling step and to average the relevant phase. The protocol is also robust to miscalibration in single qubit rotations used.

## 1. Defining the components of the PALEA model

Following the introduction of the PALEA protocol in Fig. 2(c), we aim to justify the Eqs. (2) to (4). First, we define the dynamical decoupling (DD) step D using the single qubit operators describing phased  $\pi$  rotations in appropriate subspaces:

$$D = \hat{R}_z^{12}(\phi_1)\hat{R}_x^{12}(\pi)\hat{R}_z^{12}(-\phi_1)\otimes\hat{R}_z^{01}(\phi_0)\hat{R}_x^{01}(\pi)\hat{R}_z^{01}(-\phi_0),$$
(S12)

Panel (a) shows the case without dynamical decoupling (DD), corresponding to a Floquet-type calibration, while panel (b) shows the case with a DD layer, corresponding to the MEADD-type calibration. (c) Unwanted population averaged over  $\phi$ , plotted as a function of the number of cycles. where  $\hat{R}_z^{12}$  and  $\hat{R}_x^{12}$  denote rotations around the Z and X axis in the span( $\{|1\rangle, |2\rangle\}$ ) subspace, and  $\hat{R}_z^{01}$  and  $\hat{R}_x^{01}$  are the corresponding rotations in the span( $\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$ ) subspace. The dynamical decoupling operator D is designed to keep the state in the subspace span( $\{|11\rangle, |02\rangle\}$ ):

$$D|11\rangle = -e^{i(\phi_1 - \phi_0)}|02\rangle D|02\rangle = -e^{i(\phi_0 - \phi_1)}|11\rangle,$$
 (S13)

and its action can be written as:

$$D = \hat{R}_z(\phi_0 - \phi_1)\hat{R}_x(\pi)\hat{R}_z(\phi_1 - \phi_0),$$

recovering Eq. (2).

Next, for rotations of angle  $\pi$  we have  $\hat{R}_z(\lambda)\hat{R}_x(\pi) = \hat{R}_x(\pi)\hat{R}_z(-\lambda)$  for any angle  $\lambda$ , which allows us to combine the action of D and  $\hat{U}_g$  – defined in Eq. (1) – into a single cycle unitary:

$$D\hat{U}_{g} = \hat{R}_{z}(\phi_{0} - \phi_{1})\hat{R}_{x}(\pi)\hat{R}_{z}(\phi_{1} - \phi_{0})\hat{R}_{z}(\alpha)\hat{R}_{x}(\theta)\hat{R}_{z}(\beta)$$
$$= \hat{R}_{z}(2\phi_{0} - 2\phi_{1} - \alpha - \pi)\hat{R}_{x}(\pi - \theta)\hat{R}_{z}(\beta + \pi).$$
(S14)

Finally, using Eq. (S5) we recover Eq. (3):

$$V_n(\theta, \Delta \phi) = \left| \langle 11 | \left[ \hat{R}_z(\Delta \phi) \hat{R}_x(\pi - \theta) \right]^n | 11 \rangle \right|^2,$$

where  $\Delta \phi = 2\phi_0 - 2\phi_1 - \alpha + \beta$ .

The importance of the added  $\pi$  rotation may be intuitively understood by careful inspection. Eq. (3) is a special case of Eq. (S4), and the solution proceeds identically. According to Eq. (S9), the cycle eigenvalues satisfy:


$$\cos \mu = \cos(\Delta \phi/2) \cos(\pi/2 - \theta/2) = \cos(\Delta \phi/2) \sin(\theta/2).$$
(S15)

Since the measured population depends only on  $|2\mu|$ , we may, without loss of generality, choose the positive branch  $0 \le \mu \le \pi/2$ . Taking into account the possible values of  $\cos(\Delta\phi/2)$ , this leads to the tighter bound  $\pi - \theta \le 2\mu \le \pi$ , which spans a narrow range if  $\theta \ll \pi$ .

Thus, Eq. (3) describes an iterated two-level rotation in the span( $\{|11\rangle, |02\rangle\}$ ) subspace, where the rotation axis is nearly orthogonal to the Z axis when the over-rotation angle  $\theta$  is small.

Following a derivation analogous to Eq. (S11), we obtain the signal amplitude:

$$2B = \frac{1}{1 + \left(\frac{\sin(\Delta\phi/2)}{\tan(\pi/2 - \theta/2)}\right)^2} \approx 1.$$
 (S16)

This indicates that the iterated rotation yields near-maximal contrast between the populations of  $|11\rangle$  and  $|02\rangle$  as a function of the number of cycles n for most values of  $\Delta\phi$ .

Comparing the PALEA protocol to the Floquet calibration discussed in Appendix C 3, we find that the cycle unitary in the absence of a dynamical decoupling (DD) layer is dominated by the Z component – unless the combined phase  $\phi = \alpha + \beta \ll \pi$ . Consequently, the signal amplitude in such protocols is large only near  $\phi \approx 0$  for most values of  $\theta$ , as illustrated in the simulation in Fig.S4(a) for a fixed over-rotation angle  $\theta = -0.380$ . In contrast, when a DD layer is applied – as in the MEADD protocol – the signal amplitude remains large over a wide range of  $\phi$  values and over-rotation angles, as shown in Fig.S4(b).

Averaging over the angle  $\phi$  corresponds to the "phase-averaged" aspect of PALEA. Without a DD layer, this averaging suppresses the signal significantly, resulting in a low-contrast response [Fig. S4(c)]. With a DD layer, however, the same averaging preserves high signal amplitude – this is the core advantage of the PALEA protocol.

Finally, Fig. S4(d) shows the signal averaged over both  $\phi$  and the number of cycles while sweeping the overrotation angle  $\theta$ . In the presence of a DD layer, this produces a sharp and well-defined dip, enabling precise calibration. Without a DD layer, the dip becomes shallow and less informative.

# 2. Phase tracking in the PALEA protocol

The PALEA model is defined in the laboratory frame, for reasons outlined earlier in Appendix C1. While the phase difference  $\Delta\phi$  may vary between repetitions of the experiment, it must remain constant across the

## PDF page 21

repeated cycles within a single gate sequence. Specifically, if the computational frame of each qubit is defined by the frequency of the microwave drive used to implement the required rotation, then in the laboratory frame the phase difference  $\Delta \phi$  evolves at a frequency given by  $\frac{1}{\hbar}(E_{|11\rangle}-E_{|02\rangle})$ , where  $E_{|11\rangle}$  and  $E_{|02\rangle}$  are the energies of the respective states. In most experimental setups, the phase of the microwave reference used to implement the single qubit gates is allowed to progress freely at its frequency. Without correction, this causes  $\Delta \phi$  to drift between cycles. To prevent this undesired evolution, one must track the accumulated phase and actively compensate for it. In practice, this can be achieved by regressing the microwave phase by  $\phi_i$  after each cycle, with  $\phi_1 = -\tau \tilde{\omega}_1$  for qubit  $Q_1$  and  $\phi_2 = -\tau (\tilde{\omega}_2 + \alpha_2)$  for qubit  $Q_2$ , where  $\tau$  is the duration of one cycle. In most experimental frameworks it only requires setting the virtual Z rotations attached to the CZ gate to the above values.

# 3. Solving for the expectation value in the PALEA model

Since the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the cycle with the added  $\pi$  rotation is less sensitive to  $\Delta \phi$ , averaging over  $\Delta \phi$  results in a high contrast signal from which  $\theta$  can be extracted. To justify this intuition and solve the integral in Eq. (4), we prove the following lemma:

**Lemma D.1.** For  $\hat{U} \in SU(2)$ ,  $n \in \mathbb{N}$ , the average of the matrix element

$$\left| \langle 0 | \left[ \hat{R}_z(\phi) \hat{U} \right]^n | 0 \rangle \right|^2 \tag{S17}$$

over  $\phi$  is given by

$$I_n(\theta) = 1 - \sin^2(\theta/2) \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} P_m(\cos \theta)$$
 (S18)

where  $\theta$  is such that  $\hat{U} = \hat{R}_z(\alpha)\hat{R}_y(\theta)\hat{R}_z(\beta)$  and  $P_m$  is the m-th Legendre polynomial.

*Proof.* The average can be expressed as an integral using the ZYZ Euler angle decomposition for  $\hat{U}$ :


$$I_n(\theta) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} d\phi \left| \langle 0 | \left[ \hat{R}_z(\phi) \hat{R}_z(\alpha) \hat{R}_y(\theta) \hat{R}_z(\beta) \right]^n | 0 \rangle \right|^2$$
(S19)

Note

$$\left| \langle 0 | \left[ \hat{R}_z(\phi) \hat{R}_z(\alpha) \hat{R}_y(\theta) \hat{R}_z(\beta) \right]^n | 0 \rangle \right|^2 =$$

$$= \left| \langle 0 | \left[ \hat{R}_z(\phi + \alpha + \beta) \hat{R}_y(\theta) \right]^n | 0 \rangle \right|^2$$
(S20)

utilizing Eq. (S5), and we can define:

$$\hat{R}(\phi, \theta) = \hat{R}_z(\phi)\hat{R}_u(\theta). \tag{S21}$$

# a. Expanding the integral

Following the derivation in Eqs. (S4) to (S10), the integrand of Eq. (S19) can be expanded as

$$\left| \langle 0 | \hat{R}^{n}(\phi, \theta) | 0 \rangle \right|^{2} = \left| \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \cdot S(\phi, \theta) D^{n}(\phi, \theta) S^{-1}(\phi, \theta) \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \right|^{2}$$

$$= \frac{\cos^{2}(\theta/2) - \cos(2\mu) + \sin^{2}(\theta/2) \cos(2n\mu)}{2 \sin^{2} \mu},$$
(S22)

leading to


$$I_n(\theta) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} d\phi \frac{\cos^2(\theta/2) - \cos[2\mu(\phi)] + \sin^2(\theta/2) \cos[2n\mu(\phi)]}{2\sin^2[\mu(\phi)]}$$
(S23)

#### b. Generating function

We define the generating function  $I(z;\theta)$  as a formal power series

$$I(z;\theta) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} I_n(\theta) z^n.$$
 (S24)

Substituting here  $I_n(\theta)$  from Eq. (S23), we obtain

$$I(z;\theta) = \frac{1}{4\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} d\phi \frac{\cos^2(\theta/2) - \cos[2\mu(\phi)]}{\sin^2[\mu(\phi)]} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} z^n + \frac{\sin^2(\theta/2)}{4\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} d\phi \frac{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \cos[2n\mu(\phi)]z^n}{\sin^2[\mu(\phi)]}.$$
 (S25)

The first term contains the geometric series


$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} z^n = \frac{1}{1-z}$$
 (S26)

while the second contains a related series

$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \cos(2n\mu) z^n = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left[ \left( e^{2i\mu} z \right)^n + \left( e^{-2i\mu} z \right)^n \right]$$

$$= \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{1}{1 - e^{2i\mu} z} + \frac{1}{1 - e^{-2i\mu} z} \right)$$

$$= \frac{1 - \cos(2\mu) z}{1 - 2\cos(2\mu) z + z^2}$$

$$= \frac{1 + z - 2z\cos^2 \mu}{(1 + z)^2 - 4z\cos^2 \mu}.$$
(S27)

Using these, the generating function becomes

$$\begin{split} I(z;\theta) &= \frac{1}{4\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} \mathrm{d}\phi \frac{\cos^2(\theta/2) - \cos[2\mu(\phi)]}{\sin^2[\mu(\phi)]} \frac{1}{1-z} \\ &+ \frac{\sin^2(\theta/2)}{4\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} \mathrm{d}\phi \frac{1}{1-\cos^2[\mu(\phi)]} \frac{1+z-2z\cos^2[\mu(\phi)]}{(1+z)^2-4z\cos^2[\mu(\phi)]}. \end{split}$$
 (S28)

Performing partial fraction decomposition to the integrand of the second term by treating  $\cos^2[\mu(\phi)]$  as the independent variable, we can further simplify this as

$$I(z;\theta) = \frac{1}{1-z} - \frac{z(1+z)}{1-z} \frac{\sin^2(\theta/2)}{2\pi} \times \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{d\phi}{(1+z)^2 - 4z\cos^2[\mu(\phi)]}.$$
 (S29)

Based on Eq. (S9), we have

$$\cos^{2}[\mu(\phi)] = \cos^{2}(\phi/2)\cos^{2}(\theta/2) = \frac{\cos^{2}(\theta/2)}{2} [1 + \cos(\phi)].$$
(S30)

Substituting then leads to

$$I(z;\theta) = \frac{1}{1-z} - \frac{z(1+z)}{1-z} \frac{\sin^2(\theta/2)}{2\pi} \times \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{(1+z)^2 - 2z\cos^2(\theta/2) + 2z\cos^2(\theta/2)\cos\phi}.$$
 (S31)

The remaining integral can be converted to a contour integral along the unit circle in the complex plane by defining  $w = e^{i\phi}$ , giving us

$$I(z;\theta) = \frac{1}{1-z} - \frac{z(1+z)}{1-z} \frac{\sin^2(\theta/2)}{2\pi i} \times \oint_{|w|=1} \frac{\mathrm{d}w}{-z\cos^2(\theta/2)(w^2+1) + [(1+z)^2 - 2z\cos^2(\theta/2)]w}.$$
(S32)

The integrand has two first-order poles at

$$w_{\pm} = -1 + \frac{1+z}{2z} \times \frac{1+z \pm \sqrt{1-2z\cos\theta+z^2}}{\cos^2(\theta/2)}, \text{ (S33)}$$

but only  $w_{-}$  is inside the unit circle. Using the residue theorem, we finally obtain


$$I(z;\theta) = \frac{1}{1-z} - \frac{z}{1-z} \frac{\sin^2(\theta/2)}{\sqrt{1-2z\cos\theta+z^2}}.$$
 (S34)

# c. Extracting the integrals

Utilising again the geometric series (S26) as well as the generating function

$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 2xt + t^2}} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} P_n(x)t^n$$
 (S35)

of the Legendre polynomials, we can expand the generating function (S34) as

$$I(z;\theta) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} z^n - \sin^2(\theta/2) \sum_{n,m=0}^{\infty} P_m(\cos\theta) z^{n+m+1}.$$
(S36)

Changing the summation variables from (n, m) to (n + m, m), this can be further written as

$$I(z;\theta) = 1 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left[ 1 - \sin^2(\theta/2) \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} P_m(\cos \theta) \right] z^n.$$
(S37)

From here we can immediately read that

$$I_0(\theta) = 1 \tag{S38}$$

and

$$I_n(\theta) = 1 - \sin^2(\theta/2) \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} P_m(\cos \theta)$$
 (S39)

According to the above lemma, the signal from the phase-averaged experiment is given by:

$$I_n(\pi - \theta) = 1 - \cos^2(\theta/2) \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} P_m(-\cos\theta)$$
$$= 1 - \cos^2(\theta/2) \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} (-1)^m P_m(\cos\theta)$$

using the parity condition of Legendre polynomials, and we finally recover Eq. (5).

# 4. Analyzing the robustness of PALEA

For small  $\theta$ , Eq. (5) describes a signal flipping between 0 and 1. To regularize it, we can consider


$$\tilde{I}_n(\theta) = (-1)^n \left[ I_n(\pi - \theta) - \frac{1}{2} \right] + \frac{1}{2}$$
 (S40)

instead. It can be shown to have a limiting form when  $n \gg 1$  and  $\theta \ll 1$ :


$$\tilde{I}_n(\theta) \approx \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2} J_0(n\theta),$$
 (S41)

where  $J_0$  is a Bessel function of the first kind. This expression is easier to understand and analyze than the full model. Its maximum is approximately 0.7, which is another way of confirming that higher SNR can be achieved for rotations which are nearly  $\pi$ , rather than nearly 0, seen in Fig. S4.

If a measured quantity depends on the phase as a sine,  $V \propto \sin(n\theta)$ , the phase can only be estimated up to the Nyquist frequency  $\frac{\pi}{n}$ . However, the signal following Eq. (S41) is not entirely periodic, and only assumes values between 0 and 0.35 once, for values of  $n\theta < 1.8$ . Thus if a high population of the wanted outcome state is measured after a large number of gates, one might be certain the angle  $\theta$  must be small and its absolute value may be calculated from a single phase averaged measurement. This greatly simplifies calibration, as one might fix n at a value guaranteeing required precision, and vary the gate parameters to unequivocally identify a region where  $|\theta|$  is below a certain threshold. This is why a simple sweep of a coupler pulse amplitude, shown in Fig. 2(d) works well as a quick calibration experiment.

Since the ideal  $\pi$  rotation would flip the state between  $|11\rangle$  and  $|02\rangle$ , we define the correct outcome to be  $|11\rangle$  for even n and  $|02\rangle$  for odd n. Then, the unwanted population is defined as:

$$UP_n = 1 - \frac{\text{Number of correct outcomes}}{\text{Number of the outcomes } 11 \text{ or } 02}$$
 (S42)

It is an unbiased estimator in presence of incoherent processes causing the decay to the single- and zero-excitation subspaces [56]. The variance increases as the total number of in-subspace repetitions decreases. Fig. S5(b) shows the fit of the full model [Eq. (5)] to the data, assuming that  $\theta$  depends on the coupler pulse amplitude polynomially. Fig. S5(c) shows the data and fit averaged over the number of cycles, and Fig. S5(d) the  $\theta$  values extracted as fit parameters.

The Legendre series in Eq. (5) may make it seem this model would be slow to evaluate and therefore slow to fit to the data. However, the evaluation using an appropriate version of the Clenshaw algorithm using the Legendre polynomial recurrence relation [90–92] has time complex-

ity  $\mathcal{O}(n)$  for a single n and thus  $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$  for all numbers of cycles until n and is implemented in the number Python library as polynomial.legendre.legval. One could also simply use the Bessel approximation from Eq. (S41) for fitting.

The standard error in the estimate of  $\theta$  is inversely proportional to the derivative:


$$\frac{\partial \tilde{I}_n}{\partial \theta} \approx -\frac{n}{2} J_1(n\theta) \tag{S43}$$

The Bessel function  $J_1(x)$  has an asymptotic form  $J_1(x) \sim \left(\frac{x}{2}\right)$  for x < 1 [93], so for  $n\theta < 1$ , the error in the estimate of  $\theta$  follows  $\mathcal{O}(1/n^2)$ , which is the same scaling one could reach with a sine function.

For large x,  $J_1(x)$  has an envelope given by the absolute value of the Hankel function of the first kind  $H_1(x) = J_1(x) + iY_1(x)$ , namely  $J_1(x) < |H_1(x)|$ , and  $|H_1(x)|$  has an asymptotic form  $|H_1(x)| \sim \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi x}}$  [93].

In turn, for large n we have  $\frac{\partial \tilde{I}_n}{\partial \theta} < \sqrt{\frac{n}{2\pi\theta}}$ , recovering the  $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n})$  scaling. Thus, PALEA is at its most useful with regards to precision in characterization of  $\theta$  when the product  $n\theta$  is small and calibration of the set of pulse parameters for which  $\theta=0$ . Fortunately, this is most of its use cases, because for large  $\theta$ s no error amplification is necessary.

To concretely demonstrate the sensitivity of PALEA, and verify these relationships for the full model defined in Eq. (S40), further numerical simulations of a realistic calibration procedure may be performed, as demonstrated in the following section.

#### 5. Simulating the estimation power of PALEA

We aim to illustrate the ability of PALEA to measure the angle  $\theta$  in the context of a realistic automatic calibration procedure. Such a procedure must be fast, that is, use a limited number of repetitions, each corresponding to one quantum measurement, and not require human intervention. We compare PALEA to the non-adaptive Floquet calibration using Z rotations (Fig. S3(c)) and to a MEADD procedure we could perform if we were able to set the relative phases  $\phi_0 - \phi_1$  of the dynamical decoupling layer D [Eq. (2)] in the PALEA circuit [Fig. 2(c)]. We assume a constant repetition budget of N total repetitions for each procedure. For each repetition, we calculate an ideal Born-rule probability p of measuring the target state  $-|11\rangle$  for the procedures described in this work. We then generate a random bit from a Bernoulli distribution with probability p, and additionally, with probability  $\epsilon$ , corresponding to readout error, we flip this bit. The generated bits are then averaged to obtain a data point. If a procedure requires collecting kdata points to fit them with a model, the total amount of repetitions N is split equally so that each point receives N/k repetitions.

# APPENDIX E: OUT-OF-SUBSPACE DYNAMICS

In addition to the main exchange dynamics between the  $|11\rangle$  and  $|02\rangle$  states, CZ gates might induce other unwanted transitions, such as leakage to the coupler or other neighbouring modes, if any are present. First, we will review the tools to measure the effect of those additional dynamics and then discuss how to prevent them from introducing systematic errors in the CZ gate calibration.

# 1. Measuring coupler leakage

While the extensions of the standard error amplification experiments discussed in Appendix C are less practical for leakage amplification calibration, they are effective tools for probing out-of-subspace dynamics, including coherent leakage to the coupler [18].

The goal is to observe coherent population exchange between the  $|101\rangle$  and  $|011\rangle$  states (with notation

 $|Q_1, TC, Q_2\rangle$ ) – i.e., excitation transfer from  $Q_1$  to the coupler. This two-level system, with interaction strength  $g_{1c}$ , can be described with a single Hamiltonian:

$$\hat{H}/\hbar = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & g_{1c} \\ g_{1c} & \omega_{c}(t) - \omega_{1} \end{pmatrix}, \tag{S44}$$

where  $\omega_{\rm c}(t)$  is the time dependent coupler frequency and  $\omega_1$  is the static qubit frequency. Although the coupling  $g_{1\rm c}$  depends on  $\omega_{\rm c}(t)$  as  $g_{1\rm c}(t)=\beta_{1\rm c}\sqrt{\omega_1\omega_{\rm c}(t)}$  [22], we approximate it as constant. Transforming to an interaction frame with  $\hat{H}_0(t)=(\omega_{\rm c}(t)-\omega_1)|011\rangle\langle 011|$  and corresponding unitary

$$\hat{U}_0 = e^{-i\int^t dt' \hat{H}_0/\hbar} = \exp(-\frac{i}{2}\hat{I}\left[-\omega_1 t + \int^t dt' \omega_c(t')\right]),$$
(S45)

where  $\hat{I}$  is the identity operator, yields a Landau-Zener Hamiltonian:

$$\hat{H}/\hbar = \frac{1}{2} \left[ (\omega_c(t) - \omega_1)\hat{\sigma}_z + 2g_{1c}\hat{\sigma}_x \right]. \tag{S46}$$

Analogously to Eq. (S4), the unitary  $U_c = e^{-i \int^t dt' \hat{H}/\hbar}$  can be decomposed into  $\hat{R}_z$  and  $\hat{R}_y$  rotations as:


$$\hat{U}_c = \hat{R}_z(\alpha_c)\hat{R}_x(\theta_c)\hat{R}_z(\beta_c), \tag{S47}$$

where angle  $\theta_{\rm c}$  quantifies the leakage process between the  $|101\rangle$  and  $|011\rangle$  states, and the angles  $\alpha_{\rm c}$  and  $\beta_{\rm c}$  are the Z axis rotation angles. Again, the goal is to amplify the rotation  $\hat{R}_x(\theta_{\rm c})$  for accurate measurement of the coupler leakage.

Although Slepian-shaped flux pulses reduce the leakage to the coupler [18, 52], some leakage will persist. To coherently amplify the angle  $\theta_c$ , the total Z rotation  $\alpha_c + \beta_c$  must vanish, as is evident from Eq. (S47). This can be accomplished by inserting delays or phase corrections between the CZ gates. Here, we choose the delay approach, shown in Fig. S3(a).

We perform the experiment using 30 cycles of CZ gates interleaved with delays. The measurement of  $|02\rangle$ [Fig. S7(a)] reveals the coherent exchange between the  $|11\rangle$  and  $|02\rangle$  states described earlier (vertical slices of this figure correspond to horizontal slices of Fig. 2(b) and Fig. S3(b)). In contrast, the population of  $|01\rangle$ , seen in Fig. S7(b), shows diagonal stripes. We identify these stripes as leakage to the  $|011\rangle$  state, i.e., leakage to the coupler. A Fourier transform of this signal along the delay axis reveals a frequency of 1.129 GHz, matching the qubit-coupler detuning  $(4.799 - 3.672 \,\text{GHz} = 1.127 \,\text{GHz};$ see Table S1), confirming that the peaks originate from the coupler mode. The precise delays at which the peaks occur depend on the amplitude of the coupler flux pulse approximately linearly. Note that the experiment is effectively a discrete pulsed spectroscopy, revealing a plethora of possible processes, which interfere with the coupler leakage process.

Next, we focus on characterizing the leakage error using the amplitude corresponding to the final calibrated

CZ gate, which minimizes the |02⟩ leakage. We select the delay of 7.26 ns, at which the coupler leakage process is amplified (Fig. [S7\(](#page-24-3)c)), by modelling the data using Eqs. [\(S9\)](#page-17-1)-[\(S10\)](#page-17-5), and setting ϕ = τ (ω<sup>1</sup> − ωc).

Finally, we measure the population of |01⟩ versus the number of CZ gates, shown in Fig. [S7\(](#page-24-3)d). In our case, the device is a small test chip optimized for high-fidelity operation, with the coupler as the only relevant leakage mode.

Unfortunately, methods requiring the use of X rotations – such as PALEA – could not be used to investigate coupler leakage, as the device is missing a dedicated driveline for the coupler. However, Floquet calibration methods can reveal and quantify leakage to modes outside the computational subspace – such as to the coupler – with enough measurement time available. They are however less effective when the goal is to quickly and efficiently isolate and quantify leakage to the |02⟩ state as part of the automated calibration procedure. In such cases, leakage to other spectator modes can interfere with the measurement, potentially distorting the inferred |02⟩ leakage and leading to misleading calibration outcomes. Hence, there is a need for a more robust method that selectively amplifies the target leakage process while remaining insensitive to unwanted dynamics.

# APPENDIX F: OTHER COHERENT ERRORS IN CZ GATE

#### 1. SWAP error

A CZ gate is an example of a general excitation preserving gate. In addition to two-excitation dynamics discussed above, single-excitation dynamics might additionally contribute to the overall coherent error. These dynamics may contribute to the ZZ rotation angle and the single qubit Z rotations, but those are calibrated separately. For this reason we assume the SWAP error mechanism is a unitary rotation around the XX+YY operator:

$$\mathcal{E}_{s} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & \cos(\theta_{s}/2) & -i\sin(\theta_{s}/2) & 0\\ 0 & -i\sin(\theta_{s}/2) & \cos(\theta_{s}/2) & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
(S48)

where we define SWAP angle to be  $\theta_s$ . The simplest way to calculate the gate fidelity contribution is to calculate the entanglement fidelity of the above operator with identity [95], noting that it commutes with the ideal CZ gate. Defining  $|\phi\rangle$  as a Bell state in the two copies of the system:

$$|\phi\rangle = \frac{1}{2}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle + |22\rangle + |33\rangle)$$
 (S49)

we have

$$F_e(\mathcal{E}_s) = |\langle \phi | I \otimes \mathcal{E}_s | \phi \rangle|^2 = \left| \frac{1}{4} (2 + 2 \cos \left( \frac{\theta_s}{2} \right)) \right|^2$$

$$= \cos^4 \left( \frac{\theta_s}{4} \right)$$
(S50)

Thus the SWAP contribution to average gate infidelity is


$$1 - F(\mathcal{E}_s) = 1 - \frac{4F_e(\mathcal{E}_s) + 1}{4 + 1}$$
$$= \frac{4}{5} - \frac{4}{5}\cos^4\left(\frac{\theta_s}{4}\right)$$
(S51)

We measure the SWAP angle using PALEA, preparing the  $|10\rangle$  state and using two phased- $X_\pi^{01}$  gates as the dynamical decoupling step. Thus the experimental circuit is now identical to one of the MEADD [57] circuits, and only differs by the applied phase averaging. The SWAP angle is measured after setting the width of the coupler pulse and calibrating the coupler and qubit pulses for the ZZ rotation angles and the  $|11\rangle$  -  $|02\rangle$  dynamics. The data analysis is identical to PALEA analysis, replacing the counts of 11 and 02 with counts of 10 and 01.

We find the SWAP angle below  $0.1\,\mathrm{rad}$  for all measured widths, and below  $0.04\,\mathrm{rad}$  for widths longer than 21 ns, which does not limit the gate fidelity, see Fig. S9(c). We therefore validate the design choices described in Appendix B.

# 2. Contribution to average gate fidelity from coupler leakage

In Appendix E1 we demonstrated a measurement of a coupler leakage angle  $\theta_c = 0.041 \pm 0.001$ , but we did not calculate the expected contribution to fidelity from this process. The exchange between the basis states  $|101\rangle$  and  $|011\rangle$  takes the system state from a computational to a non-computational state, making the entire process non-trace preserving. However, if the coupler is traced out, it could be understood as an incoherent process in which the state |11\rangle simply decays to  $|01\rangle$ . Making that approximation, the average gate infidelity contribution may be calculated by assuming the coupler leakage is an error channel  $\mathcal{E}_c$  such that  $\mathcal{E}_{c}(|11\rangle) = \cos(\theta_{c}/2) |11\rangle + \sin(\theta_{c}/2) |01\rangle$ , and  $\mathcal{E}_{c}(|10\rangle) =$  $\cos(\theta_{\rm c}/2)|10\rangle + \sin(\theta_{\rm c}/2)|00\rangle$ , but it does not affect the other two states. Following the derivation above, it results in the formula identical to Eq. (S51), which for the reported value of  $\theta_c$  yields  $\frac{4}{5} - \frac{4}{5} \cos^4(\theta_c/4) = (1.7 \pm 0.1) \times$  $10^{-4}$ .

This value could be understood as part of the difference between the reported error per ID gate of  $\epsilon_{\rm ID}=(4.1\pm0.2)\times10^{-4}$  and the incoherent contribution to CZ error  $\epsilon_{\rm CZ}^{\rm incoh}=6.2\times10^{-4}$  reported in Section VB, and represents another way of looking at the same process. Either the qubits hybridise with the coupler, and the excitations in that basis decay with increased rates, or the qubits leak to the coupler's excited state and then the coupler decays.

# 3. Leakage to $|20\rangle$ state

For strong couplings and short gate durations, leakage to  $|20\rangle$ , where the lower qubit's second excited state is populated, may limit the gate fidelity according to simulations. Note that this state's detuning from  $|11\rangle$  is approximately double that of the detuning between  $|01\rangle$  and  $|10\rangle$ , while the effective coupling increases by a factor of  $\sqrt{2}$ .

## PDF page 28

We could use either PALEA or the Floquet calibration with delays between the CZ gates, analogous to the one used to measure the coupler leakage shown in Fig. S7, to measure the rate of exchange between  $|11\rangle$  and  $|20\rangle$ . Note however the assumptions of the model are only satisfied if the stronger exchange in the span( $\{|11\rangle,|02\rangle\}$ ) subspace can be eliminated; otherwise the experiments need to be adjusted to take into account three-level dynamics. One such modification could be to utilize a qutrit dynamical decoupling step [96] on both transmons, with a cyclic  $X_3=X_\pi^{01}X_\pi^{12}$  gate on one and  $X_3^\dagger$  on the other, cycling through states  $|11\rangle,\,|02\rangle$  and  $|20\rangle$  — all of this is however beyond the scope of this work.

We find, using both PALEA and the delay-based amplification, that for gates shorter than 21 ns, the coherent leakage to  $|20\rangle$  can be measured to be below 0.03 rad per gate, not limiting the fidelity, while for longer gates it is too small to be measurable. At those durations, the leakage to  $|02\rangle$  cannot however be entirely eliminated, and is larger than the leakage to  $|20\rangle$ , violating the model. We conclude that a more appropriate method is to measure the total average leakage rate using the interleaved Leakage Randomized Benchmarking [72, 74], see Section V B of the main text. We note that the leakage to the lower qubit's  $|2\rangle$  in an RB circuit may originate from the direct leakage to  $|20\rangle$ , but it can also come from more complicated processes such as leakage to  $|02\rangle$ , excitation to  $|12\rangle$  and population transfer to  $|21\rangle$ .

#### **APPENDIX I: Measuring QNDness**

Quantum non-demolition (QND) measurement refers to a measurement process that preserves the quantum state of the system, meaning the qubit remains in the eigenstate to which it was projected by the readout operation [42]. This property is especially desirable for midcircuit measurements in quantum error correction, as it removes the need to reset and reinitialize the qubit afterward.

To quantify the QND nature of the readout, we define a "QNDness" metric Q, which reflects the probability that the qubit remains in its original state after measurement, independent of the measurement outcome [42, 43]:

$$Q = (Q_{\rm g} + Q_{\rm e})/2, \tag{S54}$$

with

$$Q_{g} = P(g, 0|g) + P(g, 1|g)$$

$$Q_{e} = P(e, 0|e) + P(e, 1|e),$$
(S55)

and P(a,b|c) denotes the probability that the qubit ends up in state a and is assigned outcome b, given the qubit is initially in state c. For instance, P(g,1|g) is the probability of the qubit remaining in  $|g\rangle$  but being misassigned to  $|1\rangle$ .

The aim of this section is to experimentally evaluate the QND character of our readout operations. To this end, we adopt the readout-induced leakage benchmarking (RILB) protocol introduced in Ref. [43], with modifications in the analysis, which allow us to extract not only the leakage rate, but also the QNDness  $\mathcal{Q}$ .

Directly measuring Q is challenging due to the interplay of various error mechanisms. These include assignment errors such as P(g, 1|g) and P(e, 0|e), transition

(b), (c) RILB experiment performed on  $Q_1$  and  $Q_2$  with (a) a readout operation consisting of only a readout pulse (RO) optimized for QNDness and (b) a readout operation using the shelving technique via an  $X_{\pi}^{12}$  pulse before and after the readout pulse. The experiments are performed and averaged over 500 randomized bit-flip sequences. The global and local correlations are fitted with exponential decay models.

errors such as P(e|g) and P(g|e), and leakage to higher excited states such as P(f|g) and P(f|e). The RILB protocol is designed to disentangle these effects by analyzing the decay of different correlation types between measurements. These decay rates are sensitive to leakage and transition errors but largely independent of assignment errors, making it possible to extract  $\mathcal Q$  and the leakage.

# 1. RILB experiment

The circuit diagram for the RILB experiment is shown in Fig. S11(a). The protocol consists of n repeated readout operations, interleaved with randomized bit-flips. The outcome of the nth measurement is denoted  $r_n \in \{0,1\}$ , and the applied pre-measurement operation is  $i_n \in \{0,1\}$ , where 1 indicates a bit-flip (X gate) and 0 indicates the identity operation (I gate).

To extract the local correlations between consecutive measurements, we compute the binary "flipped-or-not"

## PDF page 31

quantity:

$$o_n = r_n \oplus r_{n-1},$$

and compare it to the applied operation to get the local correlation:

$$C_n^{\text{local}} = 1 - o_n \oplus i_n$$

where  $\oplus$  denotes the XOR operation. This definition matches that of Ref. [43] up to a scaling factor.

The original RILB protocol does not allow one to extract the QNDness directly. To solve this issue, we extend the protocol to calculate another quantity from the same experimental data. We define a global correlation, which compares the state after n rounds to the initial state  $|0\rangle$ . Let

$$h_n = i_0 \oplus i_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus i_n$$

then the global correlation is defined as:

$$C_n^{\text{global}} = 1 - r_n \oplus h_n.$$

Following the computation of the correlations, we average them with respect to all possible bit-flip sequences, obtaining the expectation values  $\langle \mathcal{C}_n^{\text{local}} \rangle$  and  $\langle \mathcal{C}_n^{\text{global}} \rangle$ . While the correlations are straightforward to compute, modeling their decay as a function of n is non-trivial. In the next sections, we derive analytical models for the expectation values of both global and local correlation decay, allowing us to extract  $\mathcal{Q}$ , leakage, and seepage.

# 2. Derivation of the global correlation model

We use a simple classical hidden Markov chain model to describe the global correlation, assuming the qubit can be in one of k+2 states:  $|g\rangle$ ,  $|e\rangle$  or some leakage state  $|l_i\rangle$ , as in general, more than one leakage state may be populated during the measurement [43]. We note this assumption of Markovianity may be broken for example if the readout resonator becomes increasingly populated with photons during the experiment. We further assume the single qubit gates used are perfect. We do allow a state preparation error, but assume the qubit is never prepared in a leakage state.

We start the derivation by defining the bare transition probabilities which do not depend on the assigned outcome:

$$p_{g} = P(e, 0|g) + P(e, 1|g)$$

$$p_{e} = P(g, 0|e) + P(g, 1|e)$$

$$L_{g,i} = P(l_{i}, 0|g) + P(l_{i}, 1|g)$$

$$L_{e,i} = P(l_{i}, 0|e) + P(l_{i}, 1|e)$$

$$S_{g,i} = P(g, 0|l_{i}) + P(g, 1|l_{i})$$

$$S_{e,i} = P(e, 0|l_{i}) + P(e, 1|l_{i})$$
(S56)

as well as assignment probabilities, which we assume only depend on the state of the qubit after the readout:

$$e_0 = P(0|e)$$
  
 $e_1 = P(1|g)$  (S57)  
 $w_i = P(0|l_i)$ 

This is a somewhat risky assumption, but in practice we will see  $w_i$ -s do not enter the final formula. The  $e_i$ -s can be interpreted as readout SNR errors or overlap errors.

A hidden Markov chain model [97, 98] requires an initial state column vector  $\mathbf{v}_0$ , a transition matrix T and a row emission probability vector  $\mathbf{b}$  used to extract the required observation from the model. Thus the expected value of the global (local) correlation will be given by:

$$\langle \mathcal{C}_n^{\text{global}} \rangle = \mathbf{b}_g T^n \mathbf{v}_0,$$
  
$$\langle \mathcal{C}_n^{\text{local}} \rangle = \mathbf{b}_l T^n \mathbf{v}_0,$$
  
(S58)

and we need to find T and the two emission probability vectors  $\mathbf{b}_g$  and  $\mathbf{b}_l$  corresponding to global and local correlations. Assuming Markovianity, the global and local correlation expectations must only depend on the expected state after n steps. The emission probability vectors can be understood as linear functions from the set of states to  $\mathbb{R}$ , which are used to calculate those expectations. Without specifying the vectors  $\mathbf{b}_g$  and  $\mathbf{b}_l$ , the above equation is simply a statement that those functions are in fact linear, which is a simple consequence of the definition of the expected value.

We define a stochastic population vector  $\mathbf{v}$  containing the total probabilities of the system being in a wanted, unwanted or any of the leakage states across all possible realizations of the experiment. The wanted state for a specific realization of the experiment is defined with reference to the number of X-gates performed thus far, which is  $|g\rangle$  for an even number of X-gates preceding the last measurement or  $|e\rangle$  for an odd number of X-gates. Then,  $v^0$  is the total probability that the system is in the wanted computational state and  $v^1$  is the total probability that the system is in the unwanted computational state, that is, whatever  $v^0$  is not. Finally,  $v^{i+2}$  is the probability the system is in the i-th leakage state. Thus the length of this vector is k+2, where k is the number of leakage states communicating with  $|g\rangle$  or  $|e\rangle$ . The initial population vector is

$$\mathbf{v}_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - h \\ h \\ 0 \\ \vdots \end{bmatrix}, \tag{S59}$$

where h is the state preparation error.

Due to a non-standard definition of the basis for the vector  $\mathbf{v}$ , we need to take additional care to show that the vector of probabilities after n readouts is indeed equal to  $T^n\mathbf{v}_0$  for some square stochastic matrix T. Formally, we will demonstrate it using the principle of the mathematical induction.

## PDF page 32

Clearly, the state before any readouts is the initial state  $T^0\mathbf{v}_0 = \mathbf{v}_0$ . We need to show that, assuming the state vector after n readouts is  $\mathbf{v}_n = T^n\mathbf{v}_0$ , the vector describing the state probabilities after n+1 readouts is  $\mathbf{v}_{n+1} = T^{n+1}\mathbf{v}_0 = T\mathbf{v}_n$ .

Suppose that after the n-th readout, the wanted state is  $|g\rangle$ . Then, if there is no X-gate between the n-th and (n+1)-st readout, the vector evolves according to the transition matrix:

$$T_{0} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - p_{g} - \sum_{i}^{k} L_{g,i} & p_{e} & S_{g,0} & \cdots \\ p_{g} & 1 - p_{e} - \sum_{i}^{k} L_{e,i} & S_{e,0} & \cdots \\ L_{g,0} & L_{e,0} & \ddots & \vdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \ddots \end{bmatrix}.$$
(S60)

The unspecified matrix elements are transition probabilities from one leakage state to another, specifically  $T_0^{i+2,j+2}$  is the probability of transition from leakage state i to leakage state j. They are positive real numbers such that each column sums to 1.

If there is a flip, the basis of the vector  $\mathbf{v}$  is redefined such that its components stay the same after the flip. The basis of  $T_0$  is also transformed, which amounts to permuting the first two rows and the first two columns, resulting in the transition matrix:

$$T_{1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - p_{e} - \sum_{i}^{k} L_{e,i} & p_{g} & S_{e,0} & \cdots \\ p_{e} & 1 - p_{g} - \sum_{i}^{k} L_{g,i} & S_{g,0} & \cdots \\ L_{e,0} & L_{g,0} & \ddots & \vdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots \end{bmatrix}.$$
(S61)

The transition matrix reflects probabilities of certain transitions occurring, which depends on the presence of the X-gate used to flip the qubit state. Accounting for the X-gate being present before the readout with probability of  $\frac{1}{2}$ , and using the principle of total probability we may write  $T^{i,j} = \frac{1}{2}T^{i,j}_0 + \frac{1}{2}T^{i,j}_1$  for each matrix element, leading to the effective transition matrix:

$$T = \frac{1}{2}(T_0 + T_1) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - p - L & p & S_0 & \cdots \\ p & 1 - p - L & S_0 & \cdots \\ L_0 & L_0 & \ddots & \vdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \ddots \end{bmatrix},$$
(S62)

where  $p = \frac{1}{2}(p_g + p_e)$ ,  $L = \frac{1}{2}(\sum_i^k L_{g,i} + \sum_i^k L_{e,i})$  are average state switch and leakage probabilities,  $L_i = \frac{1}{2}(L_{g,i} + L_{e,i})$  are average leakage rates from computational subspace to leakage state i and  $S_i = \frac{1}{2}(S_{g,i} + S_{e,i})$  are average seepage rates from leakage state i to the computational subspace. The first two columns are identical after the second row, so are the first two rows after the second column. The QNDness defined earlier can be expressed in terms of these quantities: Q = 1 - p - L. The derivation for the case when the wanted state is  $|e\rangle$ , not  $|g\rangle$ , follows analogously. Thus, again following the

total probability, we can write  $\mathbf{v}_{n+1} = T\mathbf{v}_n$ , which by mathematical induction implies that  $\mathbf{v}_n = T^n\mathbf{v}_0$  for every  $n \in \mathbb{N}$ .

In addition to a left eigenvector  $\mathbf{u}_0 = [1, 1, 1, \cdots]$  with eigenvalue 1, shared by all stochastic matrices, the matrix T always has a left eigenvector  $\mathbf{u}_1 = [1, -1, 0, \cdots]$  with eigenvalue 1 - 2p - L. This can be verified by direct calculation, and is a consequence of the first two rows being identical after the second column. Additionally,  $\mathbf{v}_- = \mathbf{u}_1^T$  is a right eigenvector with the same eigenvalue, because the first two columns are identical after the second row.

Consider now the emission probability vector  $\mathbf{b}_g$  corresponding to the global correlation. Assuming the wanted state after n readouts is  $|g\rangle$ , the first component of the vector  $\mathbf{v}_n$  contributes  $1 - e_1$ , second contributes  $e_0$ , and each i-th component afterwards contributes  $w_i$ . If the wanted state is  $|e\rangle$ , the contributions change to  $1 - e_0$ ,  $e_1$  and  $1 - w_i$ . Which state is wanted is random with probability  $\frac{1}{2}$ , so the total emission probability vector is

$$\mathbf{b}_{g} = \left[1 - \frac{1}{2}(e_{0} + e_{1}), \frac{1}{2}(e_{0} + e_{1}), \frac{1}{2}(w_{0} + 1 - w_{0}), \cdots\right]$$

$$= \left[1 - e, e, \frac{1}{2}, \cdots\right]$$

$$= \left(\frac{1}{2} - e\right)\mathbf{u}_{1} + \left[\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}, \cdots\right] = \left(\frac{1}{2} - e\right)\mathbf{u}_{1} + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}_{0},$$
(S63)

where  $e = \frac{1}{2}(e_0 + e_1)$ . The expected value of the global correlation is then given by


$$\langle \mathcal{C}_n^{\text{global}} \rangle = \mathbf{b}_g T^n \mathbf{v}_0$$

$$= \left(\frac{1}{2} - e\right) \mathbf{u}_1 T^n \mathbf{v}_0 + \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{u}_0 T^n \mathbf{v}_0$$

$$= \left(\frac{1}{2} - e\right) (1 - 2p - L)^n \mathbf{u}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_0 + \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{u}_0 \cdot \mathbf{v}_0$$

$$= \left(\frac{1}{2} - e\right) (1 - 2h) (1 - 2p - L)^n + \frac{1}{2},$$
(S64)

where the third equality follows because  $\mathbf{u}_0$  and  $\mathbf{u}_1$  are left eigenvectors of T. The global correlation is therefore expected to follow a single exponential decay with offset  $\frac{1}{2}$ , regardless of the number of leakage states.

Note that this model heavily relies on proper randomization of the sequences of flips, and it is crucial to ensure the set of those sequences satisfies the required properties.

# 3. Derivation of the local correlation model

The local correlation model uses the same transition matrix T, but a different emission probability vector  $-\mathbf{b}_{l}$ . To derive it, we will consider a sum of quantities

$$P(r_{n-1} \oplus r_n \oplus i_{n+1} = 0, s_{n+1}|s_n)P(s_n)$$
 (S65)

## PDF page 33

spanning all possible cases, where  $s_n$  is the real qubit state after the *n*-th measurement. Note that only  $P(s_n)$  depends on n, according to the assumption of Markovianity.

Specifically,  $P(s_n = g) = P(s_n = e) = \frac{1}{2}(v_n^0 + v_n^1)$ , because the states  $|g\rangle$  or  $|e\rangle$  may be wanted or unwanted with probability  $\frac{1}{2}$ , while  $P(s_n = l_i) = v_n^{i+2}$ . Consequently, the two first components of the emission probability vector  $\mathbf{b}_l$  have to be equal, because we must have

$$b_l^0 v_n^0 + b_l^1 v_n^1 = A_0 \frac{1}{2} (v_n^0 + v_n^1) + A_1 \frac{1}{2} (v_n^0 + v_n^1), \quad (S66)$$

where  $A_0$  and  $A_1$  are independent of n. Unfortunately, their values depend in general on all the introduced transition and assignment probabilities, because the presence of the flip does affect the transition probability and we may no longer rely on the average leakage rates – we need to use the matrix  $T_0$  directly. We may however write

$$A = \frac{1}{2}(A_1 + A_0)$$

$$= \frac{1}{2} \sum_{s_n \in \{g, e\}} \sum_{s_{n+1}} P(r_{n-1} \oplus r_n \oplus i_{n+1} = 0, s_{n+1} | s_n),$$
(S67)

where A in general depends on all other model parameters.

We will now show that each leakage state contributes half its population to the local correlation. Consider an i-th leakage state  $l_i$ . The transition probability to another state is given by  $T_0^{i+2,j}$ . The presence of the flip does not change the state, so it does not affect the transition probability. Without the flip, the bit assignments for  $(r_{n-1}, r_n)$  as 00 and 11 contribute to the local correlation, but with the flip, the assignments 10 and 01 contribute to it. These are all possible assignments, and the probability of obtaining them is independent of the presence of the flip, thus for leakage states

$$P(r_{n-1} \oplus r_n \oplus i_{n+1} = 0, s_{n+1} = j | s_n = l_i) = \frac{1}{2} P(s_{n+1} = j | s_n = l_i)$$

$$= \frac{1}{2} T_0^{i+2,j},$$
(S68)

where the factor of  $\frac{1}{2}$  is the probability of the flip. The total expected contribution factor to the local correlation if  $s_n = l_i$  is:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{k+2} \frac{1}{2} T_0^{i+2,j} = \frac{1}{2}, \tag{S69}$$

because columns of  $T_0$  sum to 1.

We may finally define the emission probability vector for local correlation

$$\mathbf{b}_l = [A, A, \frac{1}{2}, \cdots] = (A - \frac{1}{2})[1, 1, 0, \cdots] + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}_0.$$
 (S70)

As shown before,  $\mathbf{v}_{-} = [1, -1, 0, \cdots]^{T}$  is a right eigenvector of T with eigenvalue (1 - 2p - L). Define additionally  $\mathbf{v}_{+} = [1, 1, 0, \cdots]^{T}$ , which allows us to write  $\mathbf{v}_{0} = \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{v}_{+} + (\frac{1}{2} - h)\mathbf{v}_{-}$ . Thus we obtain for the expectation value of the local correlation:


$$\langle \mathcal{C}_{n}^{\text{local}} \rangle = \mathbf{b}_{l} T^{n} \mathbf{v}_{0}$$

$$= \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{b}_{l} T^{n} \mathbf{v}_{+} + \left(\frac{1}{2} - h\right) \mathbf{b}_{l} T^{n} \mathbf{v}_{-}$$

$$= \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{b}_{l} T^{n} \mathbf{v}_{+} + \left(\frac{1}{2} - h\right) (1 - 2p - L)^{n} \mathbf{b}_{l} \cdot \mathbf{v}_{-}$$

$$= \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{b}_{l} T^{n} \mathbf{v}_{+},$$
(65)

because the vector  $\mathbf{b}_l$  is orthogonal to  $\mathbf{v}_- = \mathbf{u}_1^T$  associated with eigenvalue (1-2p-L). Thus we see that the state preparation error does not affect the local correlation. However,  $\mathbf{b}_l$  is not guaranteed to be orthogonal to any other eigenvector. In almost all cases, the matrix T will be diagonalizable and have k more eigenvectors and eigenvalues, each of which may be dependent on any remaining leakage and seepage rates, including the transition probabilities from one leakage state to another. Thus, we expect the local correlation to be a decaying function of n, with k exponential contributions.

However, typically only one leakage state is relevant. In such a case, the matrix T becomes:

$$T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - p - L & p & \frac{S}{2} \\ p & 1 - p - L & \frac{S}{2} \\ L & L & 1 - S \end{bmatrix},$$
 (S72)

where S is now total seepage rate; and gains a left eigenvector  $\mathbf{u}_2 = \left[-\frac{L}{S}, -\frac{L}{S}, 1\right]$  with eigenvalue 1 - L - S. Following Eq. (S71) we obtain


$$\left\langle \mathcal{C}_m^{\text{local}} \right\rangle = \frac{L(A - \frac{1}{2})(1 - L - S)^n + AS + \frac{1}{2}L}{L + S}, \quad (S73)$$

thus reconstructing the model from Ref. [43]. We may then estimate the leakage rate L and the seepage rate S by fitting the amplitude, offset and decay rate of the exponentially decaying data.

#### 4. Estimating the QNDness

In case of one relevant leakage state, the local correlation allows measurement of the leakage rate. This rate can then be used to calculate the QNDness  $\mathcal{Q}$  by combining the result with the global correlation decay constant estimated using Eq. (S64).

In case of more than one relevant leakage state, the total leakage rate from the computational subspace states may no longer be estimated as accurately without additional assumptions specifying certain transition rates to be 0, because the local correlation is a sum of multiple exponential decays, whose rates are eigenvalues of

## PDF page 34

T with generally complicated analytical expressions. In other words, the model is no longer fully identifiable [99] – a model with k leakage states can have up to  $k^2+k+1$  parameters, including A and all the independent rates included in the matrix T. It then generates data following a sum of k exponential decays, which can be fitted with 2k+1 parameters: a global offset, and one scaling factor and decay rate per included exponential contribution. Thus, the model becomes non-identifiable once  $k^2+k+1>2k+1$  or already for  $k\geq 2$ . It is unknown to us whether the total leakage rate from the computational subspace is an identifiable parameter without additional assumptions.

In such a case, one may bound the QNDness Q using just the global correlation rate 2p + L:

$$1 - 2p - L < Q < 1 - p - \frac{L}{2},$$
 (S74)

where the lower bound may be taken if leakage is the limiting factor.

# 5. Imperfect single qubit gates

For the purpose of the above derivation, we have assumed perfect single qubit gates. Given low errors demonstrated in the Section V A of the main text, this is a reasonable assumption, and here we demonstrate how to further test its validity. Suppose our single qubit X rotation is not perfect and the entirety of its infidelity is due to a depolarizing error  $\lambda$ . In such a case we would have  $\lambda = \epsilon_X \approx 2\epsilon_{\sqrt{X}}$  because our X gates are implemented using two  $\sqrt{X}$  gates.

Since during the RILB experiment any X rotation is immediately followed by a Z basis measurement, we can assume its action is effectively given by a classical stochastic matrix  $E_{\lambda}$ :

$$E_{\lambda} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - \lambda & \lambda & 0 & \cdots \\ \lambda & 1 - \lambda & 0 & \cdots \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots \end{bmatrix}. \tag{S75}$$

This matrix is applied to the matrix T when the X rotation is present, which occurs with probability  $\frac{1}{2}$ . The wanted state is also  $|g\rangle$  or  $|e\rangle$  with probability  $\frac{1}{2}$ , and thus the new effective transition matrix becomes:

$$T' = \frac{1}{4}(T_0 + T_1 E_\lambda + T_0 E_\lambda + T_1) = T\left(\frac{1}{2}I + \frac{1}{2}E_\lambda\right),$$
(S76)

which is a matrix structurally identical to T, with the substitution

$$p \to p + \frac{\lambda}{2} - \frac{L\lambda}{2} - p\lambda.$$
 (S77)

Thus, by assuming perfect X rotations we may overestimate the real p – and so underestimate the QNDness –

by an additive factor up to  $\frac{\lambda}{2} \approx \epsilon_{\sqrt{X}}$ , while the measured estimate for the leakage rate is unaffected.

Note that the models for global and local correlations rely on symmetry introduced by random flips. If the single qubit gate's imperfections are of a different nature, it might break that symmetry, violating the model.

We include this correction in the reported uncertainty in the QNDness.

#### 6. QND-optimized readout vs shelved readout

Fig. S11(b) shows experimental data from the RILB experiment using a QND-optimized readout configuration. This readout consists of a single rectangular pulse with a duration of 240 ns.

Silicon spin qubits offer a promising path to scalable quantum computing
due to their compatibility with industrial semiconductor manufacturing.
A key requirement for scaling quantum processors is the ability to
perform high-fidelity operations in parallel across many qubits. However,
simultaneous control remains a major challenge in silicon devices, as
fidelities typically degrade under parallel operation. Here we identify
microwave-drive-induced a.c.-Stark shifts as the dominant source of this
degradation in a five-qubit silicon spin array. We address this by introducing
a scalable mitigation protocol based solely on pairwise phase calibrations.
Using tailored control pulses on a shared control line, we achieve primitive
X and Y gate fidelities well above 99.99% for each qubit individually, with
some approaching 99.999%. Furthermore, these fidelities are preserved
above 99.99% during simultaneous operation of up to three qubits. During
parallel five-qubit operation, fidelities remain above the widely targeted
fault-tolerant threshold of 99.9%. By maintaining high-fidelity control
during simultaneous operation, shared qubit-control lines can support the
development of large-scale semiconductor quantum processors.
Quantum computing holds the potential to revolutionize fields
from cryptography to materials discovery, but building scalable,
fault-tolerant quantum processors remains a central challenge. Spin
qubits in solid-state devices1,2 are a leading platform candidate, with
a compact footprint and compatibility with industrial semiconductor
fabrication3–6, offering a route towards scaling to the millions of qubits
envisioned for fault-tolerant quantum computing architectures7.
For fault-tolerant quantum computing, gate fidelities must exceed
the threshold set by quantum error correction protocols, such as the
surface code8. While the requirement depends on the noise model,
fidelities above 99.9% are widely targeted for practical fault-tolerant
operation9,10. In scalable quantum processors, control must extend
beyond sequential single-qubit gates to the simultaneous operation

of multiple qubits, because idle qubits dephase much faster than driven
ones. In our silicon spin qubits, for example, driven coherence times
are about TR2 ≈ 100 s, while idle dephasing times are only T∗2 ≈ 10 s.
This makes sequential operations impractical at scale because minimizing qubit idle times requires simultaneous control across many qubits.
However, parallel qubit control typically degrades fidelities because
of crosstalk. Such degradation has been observed not only in spin
qubits11–13 but also in superconducting systems, where mitigation
strategies have been developed14–17. In spin qubits, the problem is
particularly severe: fidelities were recently reported to drop considerably from 99.95% to 99.5% under parallel operation of only two qubits
in silicon/silicon-germanium devices, the same platform used in this
work12. The physical origin of this degradation has remained unclear11–13.

We identify microwave-drive-induced a.c.-Stark shifts as the dominant source of fidelity degradation during parallel operation and introduce a compact calibration protocol that compensates these shifts
using only pairwise measurements. This approach avoids the exponential overhead of full crosstalk characterization and enables high-fidelity
parallel control of five qubits through a shared qubit-control line.
Tailored control pulses allow individual qubits to reach fidelities
well above 99.99%, with some approaching 99.999%, as measured
by randomized benchmarking (RB). Fidelities above 99.99% are also
sustained during simultaneous operation of up to three qubits, while
full five-qubit parallel operation maintains fidelities at the practical
fault-tolerant threshold of 99.9% or higher. This performance exceeds
previously reported fidelities in silicon spin qubits and establishes a
benchmark for scalable, high-fidelity parallel control in semiconductor
quantum processors. The use of a shared control line further reduces
wiring complexity, supporting continued scaling towards larger qubit
arrays in silicon.

High-fidelity single-qubit gates
We first operate each qubit individually. Single-qubit RB experiments
are performed on all five qubits using Kaiser-window control pulses,
shown in Fig. 2b. The Kaiser-window pulse features an excitation spectrum engineered to suppress direct MW-drive-induced qubit-qubit
crosstalk from spectral leakage26, which is essential for spin qubit arrays
operated with a shared control line. A key requirement for achieving
high-fidelity control with tailored pulses such as the Kaiser window is
to operate the qubits in a regime where the Rabi frequency scales linearly with the drive amplitude. To verify this, we measure the
Rabi frequency fR as a function of drive amplitude for all five qubits and
confirm linearity up to fR ≈ 8 MHz, as shown in Fig. 1h. Beyond 9 MHz,

The Clifford gate set is composed solely of X and Y gates, each
corresponding to a π/2 pulse, such that the fitted primitive gate fidelity matches the fidelity of the single-qubit π/2 gate (Methods). To
minimize qubit idling, where they are more susceptible to dephasing
noise, we replace the identity gate I in the gate set with 4 × X or 0 × X
(skipping the gate completely). Both replacements result in similar
fidelities, enhancing the robustness of the gate sequences against
dephasing11. For the same reason, the idle time between primitive
quantum gates is minimized to 2 ns, the hardware limit of our control
equipment (Methods). The qubit frequency and the amplitude for the
single-qubit gates are calibrated and fine-tuned before the experiment
(Extended Data Fig. 2). Following these calibrations, we measure the RB
decay for all five qubits, as shown in Fig. 2a. All qubits reach primitive
single-qubit fidelities above 99.99%, with some approaching 99.999%.
Interleaved RB28 confirms that the X and Y gates perform similarly, both
achieving fidelities well above 99.99% (Extended Data Fig. 3).
Next, we test the robustness of our high-fidelity control against
frequency detuning by measuring the RB fidelity of qubit Q3 as a function of MW–frequency offset, as shown in Fig. 2c. We find that fidelities
remain above 99.99% across a detuning window of ≈0.4 MHz. Notably,
this window is larger than the typical qubit frequency fluctuations
in our devices (≈0.1 MHz; Extended Data Fig. 4), which are primarily
caused by charge noise20,29,30.
Gate fidelity depends on a trade-off between dephasing at low
speeds and reduced driven coherence at high speeds. We evaluate this
behaviour by performing single-qubit RB experiments at varying operation speeds (Fig. 2d). The resulting infidelity increases both for slow
gates, owing to charge-noise-induced dephasing31, and for fast gates,
owing to additional MW-induced decoherence20,32–34. This trade-off is
reflected in the qubit operation quality factors, which exhibit a maximum at the optimal driving speed (Extended Data Fig. 5). For qubits Q1
to Q4, the optimal gate time results in a fidelity close to 99.999%, while
Q5 reaches an optimal fidelity of ≈99.995%, consistent with its lower
maximum spin-lock quality factor. We attribute the variations between
qubits to differences in the confinement potential, probably arising
from local fabrication, tuning conditions and material variability common in academic devices such as the one used here23,35. The proximity
of qubits Q1 and Q5 to the charge sensors acting as reservoirs may also
render them more susceptible to drive-induced decoherence, resulting
in lower spin-lock quality factors.
To quantitatively assess these trends, we compare the experimental fidelities to numerical simulations based on dephasing noise measured under idling conditions (Extended Data Fig. 4). As expected, the
simulations accurately reproduce the infidelity trends at slower gate
speeds (500 ns to 250 ns), consistent with the expected infidelity scaling 1 − F ∝ t2g for dephasing under quasi-static noise (see Methods for
details). However, at faster gate speeds, the experimental fidelities are
lower than predicted. This indicates that MW-induced noise from qubit
operations, absent during idling and thus not included in the model,
emerges at faster speeds and contributes to decoherence. The associated infidelity can be reproduced by modelling a moderate increase in
the qubit noise level caused by the applied MW signals
(Extended Data Fig. 4).

Simultaneous operation of two qubits
As a first demonstration of high-fidelity parallel control, we investigate
simultaneous operation of two qubits. We benchmark the fidelity of this
parallel control using a simultaneous RB (SRB) experiment on qubits Q3
and Q4 (ref. 14). In this experiment, independent randomized Clifford
sequences are applied simultaneously to both qubits, and the resulting
decays are fitted to extract individual gate fidelities under simultaneous
operation (Methods). Simultaneous quantum gates are implemented
Nature Physics

https://doi.org/10.1038/s41567-026-03466-2

by combining the single-qubit MW pulses for each qubit using a MW
combiner, enabling operation of multiple qubits with a shared control
line (see also Methods). Because of the a.c.-Stark effect, MW control
pulses addressed to one qubit also expose neighbouring qubits on
the shared control line to off-resonant fields, shifting their resonance
frequencies and inducing z-phase errors (Extended Data Fig. 6). These
crosstalk phase shifts are quadratic in the off-resonant drive amplitude,
with the sign opposite for each qubit in the pair, as is characteristic of
the a.c.-Stark effect. Simultaneous operations also modify the optimal MW amplitudes required for π/2 rotations, indicating residual
driven-gate errors beyond the crosstalk phase shifts. To achieve
high-fidelity simultaneous operations, both the residual rotation errors
and z-phase errors must be calibrated and compensated.
To calibrate the crosstalk phase errors, we apply a Hahn-echo
sequence to the target qubit while performing 4N X gates on another
qubit (Fig. 3a,b) and measure the resulting phase accumulation per gate
(Fig. 3c,d). The crosstalk phase accumulated on qubit Qi when operating qubit Qj is denoted as Δϕij. For Q3 and Q4, the measured crosstalk
phases are Δϕ34 = 0.0379(2) rad and Δϕ43 = −0.0026(1) rad. The discrepancy in phase shift magnitudes arises from the different power required
to drive each qubit at the same operation speed (Extended Data Fig. 5):
Q3 requires less power than Q4, resulting in a smaller off-resonant drive
and, consequently, a smaller a.c.-Stark shift experienced by Q4. These
phases are compensated during the simultaneous pulse sequence
by offsetting the MW phases during the experiment, as illustrated
in Fig. 3e. While we apply a fixed phase compensation per pulse, this
corrects the dominant crosstalk-induced phase shifts during simultaneous operation. Time-dependent phase modulation within the pulse
envelope would enable direct compensation of amplitude-dependent
phase shifts during the gate. Slower phase drifts, for example, due to
microwave-induced heating (Extended Data Fig. 7), lead to additional
phase accumulation on longer timescales36, suggesting that dynamical
phase compensation could further improve gate fidelity.
After measuring the crosstalk phase errors, we calibrate the operation pulse amplitudes, adjusting A3 and A4 for the two-qubit simultaneous operation (Fig. 3f). We apply 16 simultaneous X gates to the qubit
pairs, followed by 1 (3) simultaneous X gate(s), which flip the qubits
into |+x, +x⟩ (|−x, −x⟩ ) state to maximize sensitivity to over- or
under-rotation errors. Because the probabilities P+X and P−X for the two
sequences are equal at an optimal MW amplitude, we use an optimizer
to minimize the summed probability difference ∑Q ,Q |P+X − P−X | of

two-dimensional (2D) map of the probability difference, along with an
illustration of the optimization process.
To demonstrate the effect of our calibration scheme, we perform a
two-qubit SRB experiment on qubits Q3 and Q4, both with and without
applying the calibrations for the simultaneous operations (Fig. 3g).
In the uncalibrated case, the individual single-qubit gate pulses are
summed and applied to qubit Q3 and Q4 directly. Without calibration, Q4 still achieves a gate fidelity above 99.99%, while Q3 remains
limited to 99.9% due to a sizable uncorrected phase accumulation
Δϕ34. After applying the calibrations, both qubits reach fidelities
exceeding 99.99%.

Figure 4e,f illustrates the calibration procedure for general multiqubit simultaneous operations, demonstrated here for the three-qubit
case with qubits Q2, Q3 and Q4. The simultaneous π/2 gate duration is
set to 250 ns to reduce the peak driving amplitude. Crosstalk phases
Δϕij between each qubit pair are measured individually and compensated within the gate sequences by shifting the MW phase on each
qubit Qi by ∑j≠iΔϕij, as illustrated in Fig. 4f. The simultaneous operation
amplitudes are then calibrated using the same method described for
the two-qubit case. Figure 4g illustrates the three-qubit optimization
process, showing the driving amplitudes A2, A3 and A4, and the resulting
probability difference at each step. After approximately ten steps, the
amplitudes converge, minimizing the probability difference. Using
these calibrated phases and amplitudes, we perform a three-qubit
SRB experiment and achieve primitive π/2 gate fidelities >99.99% for
all three qubits (Fig. 4h).

Finally, we explore the limits of parallel control with our shared
control line approach by operating all five qubits simultaneously
and discuss the implications for larger arrays. To achieve this, we
calibrate all crosstalk phases and control pulse amplitudes using the
pairwise-calibration procedure described above. We also compare this
pairwise-calibration approach with a full calibration, in which the phase
accumulation on each qubit is measured while the other four are operated, and observe no difference in performance. This underscores that
the pairwise approach enables scalable and resource-efficient parallel
control across multiple qubits. Due to readout limitations (only three

Conclusions
We demonstrate high-fidelity (>99.99%) single-qubit control across all
five qubits in a silicon spin qubit array using tailored Kaiser-window
control pulses and maintain these fidelities during simultaneous operation of up to three qubits through a shared control line. Extending
to simultaneous control of all five qubits, we achieve fidelities at the
practical fault-tolerant threshold of 99.9% and suggest that, based on
the observed power scaling, this control approach is expected to extend
to approximately ten qubits within the current device architecture. In
practical quantum circuits, overall fidelity depends on the performance
of all operations, including two-qubit gates, state preparation and
measurement and idling. High single-qubit fidelity therefore remains
beneficial for circuit performance. Together, these results demonstrate
that high-fidelity parallel control across multiple silicon spin qubits

This level of parallel control is enabled by a scalable calibration
protocol that compensates for MW-induced, qubit-specific phase
shifts from off-resonant a.c.-Stark shifts. For simultaneous control
of N qubits, instead of calibrating all the 2N − N − 1 simultaneous driving combinations, only N(N − 1) pairwise calibrations are required,
avoiding the exponential overhead of full crosstalk characterization.
In general, the magnitude of these phase shifts scales inversely with
detuning, such that corrections become negligible for sufficiently
detuned qubit pairs (Extended Data Fig. 6), further reducing the calibration overhead in larger arrays. This scaling behaviour is compatible with architectures in which qubits are organized into subunits
of order ten, each operated via a shared control line and driven by
a common microwave source. These results thus provide guidance
for scalable control architectures enabling parallel operation across
multiple subunits and indicate a viable route towards scaling qubit
control in larger arrays.
In a quantum processor, two-qubit operations are also required.
The a.c.-Stark shift calibration method presented here applies when
qubits are effectively uncoupled during microwave control, such that
the resulting phase shifts correspond to additive single-qubit Z rotations. When exchange is activated during microwave driving, however,
the phase evolution is no longer restricted to simple single-qubit phase
shifts and additional conditional phases can arise38. Resonantly driven
two-qubit gates30 therefore involve microwave control while the qubits
are coupled and are expected to exhibit additional microwave-induced
conditional phases arising from a.c.-Stark effects, which would require
calibration. By contrast, exchange-controlled CZ gates implemented
with baseband pulses avoid this complication because they do not rely
on microwave driving during the interaction39.
While fidelities remain high, simultaneous five-qubit operation
is currently limited by MW-induced decoherence at fast gate speeds
and by dephasing at longer gate durations. Modest improvements
to the micromagnet design can double the driving-field gradient
(Extended Data Fig. 8 and Supplementary Section 1), enabling faster
gates at reduced power. This would mitigate MW-induced decoherence
and extend the dephasing-limited regime to higher speeds, allowing
fidelities above 99.999% at modest gate speeds of 2–3 MHz (Fig. 2d),
and supporting high-fidelity parallel control of larger qubit numbers.

To further enhance control performance and scalability, the micromagnet can be replaced by nanomagnets integrated into the gate layer
or back-end-of-line wiring. Placing magnetic structures closer to the
qubits provides stronger local field gradients40,41 and preserves gradient strength in multilayer routing architectures required for 2D or
quasi-2D spin qubit arrays42,43. Without such integration, the increased
vertical separation between qubits and magnetic structures above the
routing layers will otherwise degrade control performance44,45, limiting
the scalability of parallel qubit control.
Our shared control approach directly addresses a key scaling
bottleneck in silicon spin qubits, enabling parallel and high-fidelity
operation. Driving multiple qubits from a single impedance-matched
control line reduces wiring overhead and simplifies on-chip control
infrastructure. Combined with compact pairwise calibration and targeted device improvements, this offers a practical control strategy
for scalable silicon qubit unit cells. Crucially, the shared-control-line
architecture can be extended beyond local units to support the parallel control of multiple subarrays, with total MW drive power and
calibratable a.c.-Stark shifts as the primary constraints. Together,
these advances define a realistic path towards scalable, parallel qubit
control in silicon, which is a core requirement for the development of
large-scale quantum processors.

Methods

Device fabrication
The quantum dots were defined in a 9-nm-thick isotopically enriched
silicon quantum well with a residual Si-29 concentration of 800 ppm.
The quantum well is covered by a 30-nm SiGe buffer layer and approximately 2 nm of silicon oxide. A 15-nm Al2O3 layer was then deposited on
the wafer using atomic layer deposition. Three layers of overlapping
aluminium gates were fabricated through electron-beam lithography
and lift-off processes. The aluminium was oxidized, and the resulting
native aluminium oxide serves as an insulator between the gate layers. A micromagnet was fabricated on top of the device to provide
magnetic field gradients for qubit addressability and operations. The
micromagnet consists of a stack of 5-nm titanium and 250-nm cobalt
films and is positioned on top of the overlapping gates, separated by a
30-nm atomic layer deposition aluminium oxide layer. The micromagnet design is similar to those used in our previous devices30,33,47 with a
gap size of 400 nm. See also Extended Data Fig. 8 and Supplementary
Section 1 for more details.

Qubit control
To control the qubit energies, quantum dot confinements and tunnel
couplings, we apply d.c. voltages using D5a digital-to-analogue converters from Qblox and use baseband pulses generated by Keysight
M3201A arbitrary waveform generators to pulse the qubits between
operation and readout points. Qubit control pulses are generated by
Keysight M3202A arbitrary waveform generators and mixed with local
oscillators from a multichannel signal generator (APMS20G4) using
IQ mixers. The single-qubit operations of the five qubits are facilitated through three IQ mixers (2xMMIQ-0626HS and 1xMLIQ02181).
The IQ mixers have been carefully calibrated to minimize leakage of
local oscillator and image signals. The resulting signals are combined
in a power splitter (ZC8PD-5R263-S+), amplified (ZVA-183G-S+) and
bandpass-filtered. An MW switch (P9402C) suppresses MW leakage
outside the operation stage. The MW signal is then routed through a
low-loss superconducting NbTi coaxial line inside the dilution refrigerator, entering a designated impedance-controlled MW line on the
sample PCB. From there, the signal is transmitted via bond wires to the
quantum chip, reaching the designated MW gate (Fig. 1a).
The qubit displacement under EDSR driving can be estimated from
the simulated micromagnet gradient of 1.25 mT nm−1. Converting the
Rabi frequency fR = 3 MHz to a driving field via B1 = hfR/(gμB) with g ≈ 2
yields B1 ≈ 0.107 mT, corresponding to a displacement Δx ≈ 0.09 nm.
To perform the CROT and CZ two-qubit gates required for initializing, operating and reading out the quantum processor, the exchange
interaction between neighbouring qubits is increased from the residual exchange J < 16.6 kHz (Extended Data Fig. 1) to a few megahertz
using baseband pulses. In this device, spin–valley mixing occurs as
the exchange interaction is increased, limiting the fidelity of our CZ
two-qubit gates to <97%. Under single-qubit operation conditions,
where only residual exchange is present, the device can be tuned to
eliminate the spin–valley mixing effect across all five qubits.

Randomized benchmarking
The Clifford gate set used in the RB experiments includes only X and Y
gates, allowing the primitive gate fidelity to be directly attributed to
the fidelity of the corresponding X and Y gates, each representing a π/2
rotation. The full Clifford set decomposition is {XXXX, Y, X, YX, XY, YY,
XX, YXX, YYX, YXY, YYY, XXX, XXY, XYY, YXYY, YYXY, XXXY, YXXX, YYXX,
XYYY, YYYX, YYYXY, YXXXY, YXYYY}where the identity gate is replaced
with four X gates to prevent qubit idling. The Clifford gate set comprises
78 primitive gates when including the 4 × X identity replacement and 74
when using the 0 × X replacement. This corresponds to an average of
3.25 and 3.217 primitive gates per Clifford, respectively. In the applied
sequences, the primitive gates are interleaved with 2 ns of idle time, corresponding to the minimum delay allowed by the field-programmable
gate array for updating the MW phase.
We chose randomized sequences with a maximal Clifford gate
length of either 210 = 1,024, 213 = 8,192, 214 = 16,384 or 215 = 32,768 for
the single-qubit and the SRB experiments. For each gate length, we
randomize 35 times and average over 350 single-shot measurements
in each randomization to obtain the spin state probabilities. At the
end of each sequence, we apply a recovery gate that ensures the entire
sequence acts as either an identity operation or a X2 flip. Each sequence
then gives the corresponding flip probability Fnoflip(n) and Fflip(n). We
fit the difference F(n) = Fflip(n) − Fnoflip(n) with a exponential function
F(n) = Apn to obtain the depolarizing parameter p (refs. 48,49). The
fitted parameter A is used to normalize the raw data for plotting. We
calculate the Clifford fidelity with FC = (1 + p)/2 and obtain the primitive gate fidelity by Fp = 1 − (1 − FC)/3.25 (ref. 13). To ensure the validity
of fitting with the Apn model, we also perform fits using an additional
offset parameter, Apn + B. For all qubits, the fitted offsets satisfy ∣B∣ <
0.015. The difference in the resulting primitive gate fidelities is limited
to one decimal place smaller than our reported significant figures,
thereby validating the use of the Apn fitting formula.
Within a single randomization, we load all sequences of varying
lengths. This results in a total of approximately two million primitive
gates per loaded waveform. The loading process takes roughly 5 min,
and a full RB measurement requires about 3 h. No calibration of the
qubit resonance frequency is performed during this 3-h period, indicating highly stable qubit conditions. The number of randomizations
K = 35 is determined to provide an RB accuracy of μ = log10 ( ̂r/r) ≈ 0.1,
where r̂ is the RB-estimated average error rate and r is the theoretical
average error rate when given r = 10−4 (ref. 50). This number of randomizations is sufficient to resolve the 10−4 error rate reported in this work.
The precise measurement of a 10−5 error rate would require sequences
with longer gate length and additional data points along the decay curve.
SRB is performed by implementing single-qubit RB experiments
in parallel on multiple qubits. For each qubit, distinct RB sequences
are generated, each composed of π/2 gates with identical gate times.
These sequences are temporally synchronized, enabling a synchronized operation cycle with simultaneous operations across multiple
qubits. Due to the generation of different RB sequences, the number of primitive gates in each sequence may vary across qubits. Any
additional primitive gates are applied either through simultaneous
pulses on a subset of qubits or via individual single-qubit pulses. The
difference in the number of primitive gates among individual qubit
sequences can be as large as 200 for a sequence containing 10,000
Clifford gates.

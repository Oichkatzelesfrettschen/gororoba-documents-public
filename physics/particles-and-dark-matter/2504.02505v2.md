Abstract
The ALICE Collaboration reports its first LHC Run 3 measurements of charged-particle pseudorapidity density at midrapidity in Pb–Pb collisions at a centre-of-mass energy per nucleon pair of
√
sNN = 5.36 TeV. Particle multiplicity in high-energy collisions characterises the system geometry,
constrains particle-production mechanisms, and is used to estimate initial energy density. Multiplicity also acts as a reference for subsequent measurements as a function of centrality. In this letter, for
the first time, charged particles are reconstructed using the upgraded ALICE Inner Tracking System
and Time Projection Chamber, while the collision centrality is determined by measuring chargedparticle multiplicities with the Fast Interaction Trigger system. Pseudorapidity density, dNch /dη, is
presented, averaged over events, for various centrality classes. Results are shown as a function of
pseudorapidity and the average number of participating nucleons (⟨Npart ⟩) in the collision. The average charged-particle pseudorapidity density (⟨dNch /dη⟩) at midrapidity (|η| < 0.5) is 2010 ± 53
for the 5% most central collisions. The value of ⟨dNch /dη⟩ normalised to ⟨Npart ⟩/2 as a function of
√
sNN follows the trend established in previous measurements in heavy-ion collisions. Theoretical
models based on mechanisms for particle production in nuclear collisions that involve the formation
of quark–gluon plasma medium and models based on individual nucleon–nucleon interactions are
compared to the data.

Introduction

The multiplicity of produced particles is a primary observable used to characterise the system created
in a high-energy heavy-ion collision and its geometry. This quantity constrains the particle production
mechanisms and is employed to estimate the initial energy density of the system [1, 2].
The ALICE apparatus has been upgraded during the Long Shutdown 2 (LS2) of the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) [3, 4]. Key upgrades include a new tracking system in the central barrel [5, 6], new forward
trigger detector [7], continuous readout [8], and a novel online-offline software framework (O2 ) [9] to
handle both data reconstruction and analysis. The upgraded ALICE detector resumed data-taking in
√
2022 with proton–proton (pp) collisions at a centre-of-mass energy s = 0.9 and 13.6 TeV and lead–
√
lead (Pb–Pb) collisions at a centre-of-mass energy per nucleon pair sNN = 5.36 TeV.
This paper presents not only the first measurements by ALICE in Pb–Pb collisions at the highest energy
at the LHC but also provides important validation of the upgraded apparatus and software. In detail,
the pseudorapidity density of primary charged particles is presented as a function of pseudorapidity,
exploiting also their dependence on the overlapping area of the nuclei, defined by centrality. In accordance with the ALICE definition, primary particles are defined as charged particles with a mean proper
lifetime τ larger than 1 cm/c, which are either a) produced directly in the collision, or b) from decays
of particles with τ smaller than 1 cm/c, excluding particles produced in interactions with material [10].
Moreover, charged-particle pseudorapidity density is studied as a function of the number of participating
nucleons in the collision, as determined using the Glauber model [11–13]. Where available, results are
compared to those at the same collision energy by the CMS experiment [14], or to results from different
energies and colliding systems by experiments at LHC [15–21] and at Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider
(RHIC) [22–26], as well as earlier experiments at CERN [27]. To interpret the underlying particle production mechanisms, results are compared to models that assume quark–gluon plasma (QGP) formation
with hydrodynamic evolution of the system, and models that do not incorporate QGP.
The paper is organised as follows: Sec. 2 presents the experimental setup focusing on the upgraded
detectors, data samples and the used analysis method. Section 3 describes the correction procedure in
detail, while systematic uncertainties are discussed in Sec. 4. Finally, results are presented in Sec. 5, and
the conclusions are summarised in Sec. 6.

2

Experimental setup

2.1

ALICE detectors in LHC Run 3

The ALICE detector [3, 4] consists of a central barrel contained in a solenoidal magnet (B = 0.5 T) and
a forward muon system with a dipole magnet providing a total bending power of 3 T m. During LS2
the central barrel detectors have been upgraded and designed for efficient tracking in the high trackdensity environment of heavy-ion collisions, covering transverse momenta (pT ) from ∼ 100 MeV/c to
∼ 100 GeV/c with excellent hadron and electron identification capabilities.
The new Inner Tracking System (ITS2) consists of seven layers of ALPIDE monolithic active pixel
sensors (MAPS) [5]. The ITS2 has improved pointing resolution owing to its reduced distance to the
interaction point and better primary vertex position resolution with respect to that of the ITS used in the
LHC Runs 1 and 2. It can also handle the hit densities produced from Pb–Pb collisions at rates up to
50 kHz in nominal operating conditions.
In the radial direction, the ITS2 is followed by the Time Projection Chamber (TPC) extending from
0.85 m to 2.5 m in radius over a length of 5 m [6, 28]. The TPC was upgraded from multiwire proportional
chambers to readout chambers based on Gas Electron Multiplier (GEM) foils that reduce the ion backflow
and resulting space charge in the drift volume to a correctable level for nominal operating conditions [29].

A set of new forward detectors form a Fast Interaction Trigger (FIT) system [7], which provides precise
timing for continuous readout and is also used for centrality estimation, event selection, and collision
time determination. This analysis uses the FIT time-zero (FT0) detector which consists of two arrays of
fast Cherenkov counters featuring unique timing capabilities: FT0A, covering the pseudorapidity interval
3.5 < η < 4.9, and FT0C, covering −3.3 < η < −2.1.
Furthermore, the readout infrastructure was completely renewed to support the continuous readout of the
core detectors [8]. In nominal operating conditions, each TPC drift time period of ≈ 83 µs contains,
on average, five Pb–Pb events. Thus, a continuous, non-triggered readout strategy was implemented,
combined with online data compression for the upgraded readout and data acquisition system. In order
to synchronise the continuous data stream across all readout and processing branches, the data stream is
divided into time frames of a nominal length of 32 LHC orbits (≈ 2.8 ms) [30].
2.2

Data sample and analysis method

√
This analysis uses a sub-sample of data from Pb–Pb collisions at sNN = 5.36 TeV recorded by ALICE
in 2023. The analysed data sample consists of 1.1 × 109 minimum bias Pb–Pb events, selected from runs
with interaction rates ranging from 12 to 43 kHz. This corresponds to an integrated luminosity (Lint ) of
about 130 µb−1 , estimated before applying the event selection criteria.
The transition to continuous readout necessitates changes in the reconstruction strategy [31]. In the
continuous readout [8], the association of tracks (reconstructed trajectories of charged particles) to the
collision vertex relies on track timestamps in relation to FIT vertex signals and, thus, is affected by
the time resolution. Previously, in the triggered readout, this association was unambiguous, with the sole
exception of same-bunch pileup. As a result, now the full time frame, containing several collisions, needs
to be reconstructed together. By construction, this introduces a track-to-vertex association ambiguity.
Depending on the interaction rate, a single track with large enough time uncertainty may be compatible
with several recorded collision vertices. The fraction of tracks that have a better compatibility with
a vertex different from the most probable one, based on the distance of the closest approach (DCA)
criteria, is at sub-percent level for the 2023 data. Therefore, track ambiguity is neglected for the tracks
that are used in this analysis.
A set of requirements is used to select the minimum bias events. The vertex trigger is produced by
FIT when both FT0A and FT0C arrays detect at least one hit in the nominal collision time window,
synchronised with the LHC clock. The time difference between the two signals defines the position of the
collision vertex. Collisions are removed based on their proximity in time to the ITS readout frame border
and the time frame border. This avoids partially recorded events. Events with a longitudinal position
within 10 cm of the nominal interaction point are selected, based on the typical width of the vertex
position Gaussian distribution of ∼ 5 cm. Additionally, the events where the vertex has no contributing
global tracks (i.e. tracks that combine hits in ITS and TPC) and events where the vertex position estimate
from FIT is more than 1 cm away from the tracked vertex position are ignored due to lower overall quality.
The effect of the detector occupancy on the tracking efficiency is also considered, as a consequence of
the continuous readout approach combined with the high interaction rate. The occupancy effect is similar
to that of event pile-up, as the collision under consideration may share the detector readout window with
collisions with very high multiplicities, resulting in a decrease in tracking capability in both ITS and
TPC. An occupancy of less than 500 tracks has been chosen in order to maintain approximately uniform
tracking efficiency across the full sample.
After applying the event selection criteria described above, the data sample consists of about 0.2 × 109
events. The selected event sample is then classified into centrality classes using the raw amplitudes of
signals in the FT0C detector, which are proportional to the number of charged particles detected by the
FT0C. Following the method developed previously in Runs 1 and 2 measurements [32, 33], the measured

Systematic uncertainties

The contributions of the different sources of systematic uncertainties associated with the measurements
of charged-particle pseudorapidity density are summarised in Table 1. A study is performed based on
occupancy of more than 500 tracks other than the nominal one as described in Sec. 2.2, and we observe
that the results are consistent with each other. Therefore, the occupancy effect on the final measurements
is not added to the systematic uncertainty. The details about the different conditions varied in the analysis
to estimate the systematic uncertainties are described below.
– Acceptance and efficiency: This uncertainty arises from the limited detector efficiency and nonuniformity in azimuthal acceptance of ITS and TPC. It is estimated by measuring the dNch /dη in
six different azimuthal sectors and comparing them to the measurement in the full azimuth. This
uncertainty is considered as correlated over both η and centrality.
– zvtx dependence: The effect of detector acceptance is also studied by carrying out the analysis for
different slices of the z position of the interaction vertex distribution and comparing the results
with the one obtained with nominal zvtx cut (|zvtx | < 10 cm). This uncertainty is uncorrelated over
both η and centrality.
– pT extrapolation: Below 100 MeV/c, the charged-particle reconstruction efficiency sharply drops

because of the bending of charged-particle trajectories under the effect of the magnetic field and,

effect of the undetected particles at low pT , the production of primary charged particles with
pT < 100 MeV/c is varied in the event generator by a conservative amount (+100% and −50%),

– Strangeness content: The uncertainty associated with the correction for the contamination from
weak decays of strange hadrons is determined by varying the strange particle content in the event
generator. The yield of strange particles in data is found to be 10% larger than in simulation, and
this discrepancy is varied by ±20% to estimate the uncertainty. This uncertainty is considered
correlated over both η and centrality.
– Particle-species composition: The composition of the produced particle species affects the determination of efficiency because different particle species have different efficiencies that depend on
the applied effective pT thresholds and the decay kinematics. The influence of this uncertainty is
estimated by changing the relative abundances of charged kaons, protons and other charged particles with respect to charged pions by ± 30% in the simulation. This uncertainty is correlated over
both η and centrality.
– Track selection: The systematic uncertainty related to the track selection criteria is determined by
varying the track matching requirements in the ITS detector. In particular, the nominal analysis
requires at least one hit in the first three ITS layers. Two alternative criteria are tested: (a) requiring
at least one hit among all the ITS layers, and (b) requiring one hit on all the ITS layers. This
uncertainty is uncorrelated over both η and centrality.
– Centrality determination: The centrality determination introduces an uncertainty via the fitting of
the FT0C amplitude distribution to the hadronic cross section due to the contamination from electromagnetically induced reactions at small multiplicities. The uncertainties associated with centrality determination and ⟨Npart ⟩ calculation are evaluated by varying certain parts of the Glauber
fitting procedure. In particular, in the default FT0C estimator, the signal region in which data and
the NBD-Glauber fit are matched is up to the top 90% of the hadronic cross section, while in the
systematic variations, it is evaluated on the full centrality range. A second variation is done to
allow the parameter k of the NBD to be free, instead of fixed to 1.5 as in the default FT0C estimator. Finally, σINEL and a, R in Eq. (2) are varied within their systematic uncertainty values. These
variations result in an uncertainty on dNch /dη of 0.1% for the most central collisions, increasing
towards the peripheral collisions and reaching 11.5% for the 80–90% sample. This uncertainty is
correlated over η but uncorrelated over centrality.
– Estimation of event and signal loss corrections: These corrections depend on how the generator
level centrality in MC is estimated. The event and signal loss corrections are obtained using
two independent proxies for generator-level centrality: the impact parameter distribution and the
charged-particle multiplicity distribution within the FT0C acceptance. The uncertainty associated
with this estimation is then evaluated from the difference between the correction factors obtained
using these two methods. This difference results in an uncertainty on dNch /dη of 3.5% for the
most peripheral collisions, while being negligible for the other centrality classes. This uncertainty
is correlated over η but uncorrelated over centrality.
The contributions of different sources of correlated and uncorrelated uncertainties are summed in quadrature to obtain the total systematic uncertainty in each centrality interval, which varies from 2.6% in the
most central collisions to 12.4% in the most peripheral collisions.

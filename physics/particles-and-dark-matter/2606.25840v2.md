Abstract
A search for long-lived
√ particles decaying into muons is performed using protonproton collisions at s = 13.6 TeV, collected by the CMS experiment at the LHC in
2022 and 2023, corresponding to an integrated luminosity of 62.4 fb−1 . The data were
collected using dedicated dimuon triggers with low transverse momentum thresholds, recorded with a high-rate data scouting trigger stream. This data stream retains
a reduced amount of information at the high-level trigger, to explore otherwise inaccessible phase space at low multimuon invariant mass and nonzero displacement
from the primary interaction vertex. No significant excess of events above the standard model prediction is found. Upper limits on branching fractions at 95% confidence level are set for a wide range of mass and lifetime hypotheses in several beyond
the standard model frameworks, where the Higgs boson decays into long-lived dark
photons or into dark partons that produce showers containing long-lived particles, or
where a long-lived scalar resonance is produced from the decay of a b hadron. The resulting constraints improve and extend existing ones in large regions of the parameter
space.

Introduction

The origin of dark matter remains one of the outstanding puzzles in particle physics and cosmology, in spite of the cosmological evidence of its existence [1–4]. Dark matter is expected
to interact very weakly, if at all, with standard model (SM) particles, except via gravity. This
introduces the possibility of a hidden (dark) sector of matter [5–7]. Particles in the dark sector
would interact with the SM particles only via weakly interacting mediators.
One dark-matter scenario, referred to as the hidden Abelian Higgs model (HAHM), involves a
spontaneously broken dark U (1)D gauge symmetry, mediated by a dark photon, Z D [6]. In this
scenario, the only renormalizable interaction with SM particles occurs via the kinetic mixing
of a dark photon with a hypercharge gauge boson. In addition, if a dark Higgs mechanism is
responsible for the spontaneous breaking of the U (1)D gauge symmetry, then the dark Higgs
boson (H D ) has a renormalizable coupling to the 125 GeV SM-like Higgs boson (H), resulting
in mixing between the two physical scalar states. Thus, the hidden sector may interact with the
SM sector either through the hypercharge portal via the kinetic mixing parameter ϵ, or through
the Higgs portal via the Higgs mixing parameter κ. The dark photon Z D may also mix with
the SM photon (γ) and the Z boson through the hypercharge portal. If there are no hiddensector states below the Z D boson mass, the Z D decays exclusively into SM particles, with a
sizable branching fraction to leptons, where the coupling of the SM fermions to the Z D boson
is proportional to ϵ. The Z D boson is expected to be long-lived if ϵ ≲ 10−4 , with a mean proper
Z
decay length cτ0 D ≳ 1 µm, proportional to ϵ−2 . In this regime, the decays H → ZZ D and
H → Z D Z D through the hypercharge portal have negligible branching fractions. The diagrams
in Fig. 1 illustrate the production of a pair of Z D bosons in a Higgs boson decay.

Figure 1: Diagrams illustrating an SM-like Higgs boson (H) decay to four fermions ( f ) via two

In other hidden-sector models [8], a new, confining dark gauge group is introduced, which
can produce events with multiple nonisolated dark particles, referred to as “dark showers”,
analogous to phenomena in the quantum chromodynamics sector of the SM. A spontaneously
broken U (1) gauge symmetry is involved, mediated by a dark photon A′ that couples to the SM
photon. The confining dark gauge group dynamically breaks the hidden-sector SU (2) ⊗ SU (2)
flavor symmetry, resulting in the addition of a set of three dark pions (π̃ 1 , π̃ 2 , and π̃ 3 ) and a
heavier dark meson, η̃ . In these models, the SM Higgs boson can decay into a pair of dark
partons ΨΨ, which then undergo showering and hadronization within the dark sector. On
average, five dark mesons per event are produced. Different scenarios are considered: a model
where the dark photon A′ is long-lived, referred to as Scenario A [8], and a model where the

Constraints have been placed on visible dark-photon decays by beam dump [9], fixed-target [10],
e + e − collider [11], and rare-decay experiments [12], as well as by the LHCb [13–15], ATLAS [16], and CMS [17–21] experiments at the CERN LHC.
Other scenarios of physics beyond the SM (BSM) are also considered that may produce a longlived, low-mass resonance decaying into a muon pair. In one of the minimal extensions of the
SM a singlet scalar field ϕ is added, which mixes with the Higgs boson with a mixing angle θ
and thereby couples to all SM fermions [22, 23]. The ϕ boson is expected to be long-lived under
the hypothesis of weak couplings to SM fermions, corresponding to θ ≲ 10−3 . Such a scalar
resonance can be produced in decays of b hadrons, h b → ϕX. This process is depicted in Fig. 3.
Constraints on this model have been previously set by the CHARM [24], LHCb [25, 26], and
CMS [18] experiments.

The CMS detector

The central feature of the CMS apparatus is a superconducting solenoid of 6 m internal diameter providing a magnetic field of 3.8 T. Within the solenoid volume are a silicon pixel and strip
tracker, a lead tungstate crystal electromagnetic calorimeter, and a brass and scintillator hadron
calorimeter, each composed of a barrel and two endcap sections. Forward calorimeters extend
the pseudorapidity (η) coverage provided by the barrel and endcap detectors. Muons are reconstructed using gas-ionization detectors interleaved with the layers of the steel flux-return
yoke outside the solenoid. A global “particle-flow” (PF) algorithm [29] aims to reconstruct all
individual particles in an event, combining information provided by the all-silicon tracker and
by the calorimeters with data from the gas-ionization muon detectors. The pixel tracker con-

sists of four concentric barrel layers at radii of 2.9, 6.8, 10.9, and 16.0 cm, and three disks on each
end at distances of 29.1, 39.6, and 51.6 cm from the center of the detector [30]. The region between 20 and 116 cm is occupied by the silicon strip tracker. Four barrel layers, supplemented
by three disk in the endcaps, compose the tracker inner barrel and disks, which extends the
radius up to 55 cm. Beyond this range, the tracker outer barrel and the tracker endcaps are
situated, adding six layers and nine additional disks at each end. A more detailed description
of the CMS detector, together with a definition of the coordinate system used and the relevant
kinematic variables, can be found in Refs. [31, 32].
A two-level trigger system is used to select events of potential physics interest. The first level of
the CMS trigger system (L1) [33], composed of custom hardware processors, uses information
from the calorimeters and muon detectors to select events of interest in a fixed time interval of
about 4 µs. The HLT processor farm further decreases the event rate from around 100 kHz to
a few kHz, before data storage. A more detailed description of the CMS trigger system can be
found in Refs. [34, 35].
In addition to the standard HLT selection streams, a dedicated set of triggers exists that allows
the exploration of additional phase space. This approach is referred to as data scouting [27].
The scouting trigger algorithms used in this search select events containing muon pairs with
a dimuon invariant mass greater than the muon mass (mµµ ≳ 2mµ ) at a rate of a few kHz. In
order to allow the large rate within an acceptable bandwidth, the event size is reduced by up
to a factor of 1000 by only retaining limited information, as reconstructed at the HLT.

Data sets and triggers

The standard trigger streams typically select events containing at least one particle (e.g., a lepton) or jet with large pT . Thus, such triggers are not necessarily ideal in a generic search for
light, narrow resonances decaying into two muons with pT ≲ 10 GeV. Muons are reconstructed
at the HLT by combining the information from the tracker and the muon detectors. The retained
limited information includes their four-momenta, the number of hits left in the tracker and in
the muon detectors, and detailed information on the muon tracks. These muon tracks are used
in pairs to form dimuon vertices, considering all possible pairs. In the following, these vertices
are called secondary vertices (SVs), and they may be either displaced from the PV or not. Candidate pp interaction vertices are formed using tracks reconstructed from hits only in the pixel
detector, and the candidate vertex with the largest value of summed track p2T is taken to be the
PV of the pp interaction. Events in the scouting data sets contain information on the normalized χ2 of all reconstructed vertices, together with their position with respect to the nominal
interaction point.
Between 2018 and 2022, the CMS muon scouting triggers [36] were updated, broadening the
accessible phase space and increasing the amount of information retained for each event. The
main improvement arises from the removal of the pixel hit multiplicity requirement, which
previously prevented any muon with <2 pixel hits from being accepted by the scouting triggers. As a result, the muons with lxy > 11 cm are now accessible for the first time in a search
using scouting data. Additionally, more detailed information on tracks and PF candidates is
retained and used to improve the background rejection, as described in Section 5.1.
Events are preselected at the L1 trigger with at least two muons passing requirements on the
pT , η and charge of each muon and on their angular separation, defined as ∆R(µ 1 , µ 2 ) =
√

Monte Carlo simulation

The SM background is estimated directly from data in the dimuon (mµµ ) or four-muon (m4µ )
invariant mass spectrum, parameterized by smooth analytical functions. Thus, the search does
not rely on MC simulation in order to estimate the background.
On the other hand, MC simulations of BSM signals are used to interpret the results of the search.
In particular, a model of Higgs boson production via the dominant mode of gluon-gluon fusion
is simulated, with H → Z D Z D , where Z D is a long-lived resonance and at least one Z D decays
into a pair of OS muons. The phenomenology of this model is defined by four parameters:

• the kinetic mixing ϵ;
• the mass of the dark photon, mZ D ;
• the Higgs portal coupling, κ; and
• the mass of the dark Higgs boson, mH D .
In the simulation used in this search, mH D and κ are fixed to 400 GeV and 0.01, respectively, in a
regime where H → H D H D decays are kinematically forbidden. Instead, the Higgs boson is set
to always decay into a pair of Z D bosons, which in turn decay into SM fermions with branching fractions that depend on their mass [6]. The branching fraction for Z D decays into muon
pairs varies from about 50% at a mass of 0.5 GeV to about 9% at a mass of 50 GeV. The lifetime
of the Z D boson for a given mass hypothesis is determined by ϵ. In this paper, dark photons

7

Results and interpretation

Unbinned maximum likelihood fits to the data are performed simultaneously in all search bins,
under either background-only or background+signal hypotheses, using background and signal
models and uncertainties described in Section 6. Additional log-normal constraint terms are
used to account for the uncertainties in the signal yields, when considered (Section 7.1).
No significant peak-like structures are observed in data. The background+signal fits are used
to set 95% confidence level (CL) upper limits on the branching fractions for the signal models
under consideration. Limits are set using a modified frequentist approach, employing the CLs
criterion [49–51] within the CMS statistical analysis tool C OMBINE [52], which is based on the
R OO F IT [53] and R OO S TATS [54] frameworks. These limits are then used, in conjunction with
the theoretical cross section calculations, to exclude ranges of masses and/or lifetimes for the
BSM particles of the signal models.

Summary

A search for displaced multimuon resonances has been performed using proton-proton collisions at a center-of-mass energy of 13.6 TeV, collected by the CMS experiment at the LHC in
2022 and 2023, corresponding to an integrated luminosity of 62.4 fb−1 . The data were collected
using a dedicated dimuon trigger stream with low transverse momentum thresholds, recorded
at high rate by retaining a reduced amount of information at the high-level trigger, to explore
otherwise inaccessible phase space at low multimuon invariant mass and nonzero displacement from the primary interaction vertex. No significant excess above the standard model
prediction is found. The data are used to set constraints on a wide range of mass and lifetime
hypotheses for various scenarios of physics beyond the standard model. Three sets of models
are considered: the hidden Abelian Higgs model, where the Higgs boson decays into a pair of
long-lived dark photons; dark-shower models, where dark showers originate from the Higgs
boson decays into dark quarks; and models where a long-lived scalar resonance is produced
from the decay of a b hadron. In models with a b hadron, the improved trigger and analysis
strategy compensate for the smaller data set, leading to constraints comparable with the previous results. For other models, the constraints obtained are instead the most stringent to date in
a large fraction of the explored parameter space. In the hidden Abelian Higgs model, the most
stringent limits to date are obtained for dark-photon proper decay lengths greater than 1 cm
and dark-photon masses less than 5 GeV. In the dark-shower models, the most stringent limits
to date are set for proper decay lengths larger than 10 cm.

Abstract
A search for new scalar particles of O(GeV) mass in exclusive final states with muons
and light hadrons is presented. The analysis uses proton-proton collision data produced at a center-of-mass energy of 13 TeV collected by the CMS experiment at the
CERN LHC in 2016–2018, corresponding to an integrated luminosity of 138 fb−1 . The
search targets exotic decays of the Higgs boson to a pair of prompt or long-lived identical scalar particles with proper decay lengths cτ up to 100 mm and masses within
the range of 0.4–2.0 GeV. This mass window corresponds to a unique parameter space
where hadronic decays of these particles mostly result in a pair of light hadrons. The
considered experimental signature is a collimated pair of muons and another pair of
charged kaons or pions, each of which may be prompt or displaced. The analysis
improves the sensitivity to very light scalar boson masses and demonstrates a novel
approach to probe hadronic decays of light scalar bosons. Upper limits on the branching fraction of the Higgs boson to scalar bosons at the level of O(10−4 ) are obtained
for several scalar boson masses between 0.4 and 2.0 GeV, with proper decay lengths
of up to ∼1 mm.

Introduction

The discovery of the Higgs boson (H) by the ATLAS and CMS experiments [1, 2] was a crowning achievement for the standard model (SM) of particle physics, which describes the fundamental particles and their interactions with remarkable precision. The discovery gave rise to
the quest to study the properties of this new particle in detail and to confirm or refute its role in
the Brout-Englert-Higgs mechanism of electroweak symmetry breaking [3–6] and its Yukawa
couplings to fermions.
Many measurements by the ATLAS and CMS Collaborations have since confirmed this theoretical picture with increasing precision [7, 8]. However, only a subset of the couplings of the
Higgs boson to SM particles could so far be confirmed, still leaving room for up to O(10%)
deviations from the SM expectation. Furthermore, the Higgs boson may still decay to invisible particles with branching fractions up to 10.7 and 15.0%, as constrained by direct searches
from the ATLAS [9] and CMS [10] Collaborations, respectively. Independent global fits of the
SM Higgs boson couplings, with the possibility of the Higgs boson decaying to a potentially
visible but undetected final state, have been performed by the ATLAS [7] and CMS [8] Collaborations. These fits allow at 95% confidence level (CL) the possibility of up to 12 and 16%,
respectively, of the Higgs bosons decaying in this way.
Theoretically, deviations from the SM scalar sector predictions driven by beyond-the-SM (BSM)
physics can arise in various ways. A particularly important example consists of the SM singlet
( H † H ), where H is the SM Higgs field, coupling to a new real scalar field S, itself a singlet,
with renormalizable interactions (µ̃S + λS2 )( H † H ), where µ̃ is a mass parameter and λ is a
dimensionless coupling. The scalar field S may then interact with a so-called dark sector of
new BSM particles and interactions that also do not carry SM charges. The mixing between
the H and S fields originates from the renormalizable interactions, also referred to as the Higgs
portal, through which the presence of the dark sector may affect the measured SM Higgs boson
properties. In practice, the dark-sector particles acquire an effective coupling to the Higgs
boson, yielding a possibly rich phenomenology, depending on their nature and interactions.
In this paper, we consider a minimal Higgs portal scenario with the real singlet field S giving
rise to an extra scalar boson S that mixes with the SM Higgs boson, as considered in more detail
in Refs. [11–13]. In particular, light scalar bosons of O(1 GeV) appear in several SM extensions
including cosmic inflation models [14–16], supersymmetry [17, 18], dark matter models [19, 20],
and cosmological solutions to the hierarchy problem [21]. Many searches have already been
conducted for a variety of signatures that result from such scenarios, but usually in the case
where the scalar boson is heavy [22, 23]. For the search presented here, we explore the lowmass region where the mass of the S particle mS ≲ 2 GeV, such that hadronic decays of the S
particle do not result in collimated jets of multiple hadrons, but rather lead dominantly to the
exclusive S → K + K − and S → π + π − decays, respectively, above and below the mS = 2 mK ±
kinematic threshold. These decays can result in a decay length which is measurable in the
detector. In order to maintain the generality of the search, we consider the lifetime of S as a free
parameter of the model.
A search for low-mass scalar bosons in such exclusive decays was originally proposed in
Ref. [24] for the LHCb Collaboration, using fully hadronic decays. In CMS analyses, without
dedicated particle identification methods for high-momentum charged kaons, a fully hadronic
final state from inclusive Higgs boson production is overwhelmed by hadronic backgrounds.
Instead, we require one of the light scalar bosons to decay to muons, providing a signature that
can be triggered efficiently. We consider the H → SS → µ + µ − h + h − signal process, where
the hadron h ± can be either a charged pion or kaon. A diagram of this process is shown in

The considered final state has been probed in several prior searches. The search for dimuon
resonances at low mass using the CMS scouting data set [26] is sensitive to the H → Z D Z D →
µ + µ − + X decay chain, reaching limits down to 10−4 on the branching fraction of the Higgs
boson to the considered intermediate dark Z D vector boson, depending on the lifetime and the
mass of this boson. Of note is that a substantial mass range below ∼1.1 GeV was masked out
in this search where the presence of SM resonances hindered reliable predictions of the background. A complementary search for a pair of new bosons with masses as low as 0.21 GeV in
events with a four-muon final state was also performed by the CMS Collaboration in Ref. [27].
A search for anomalous showers resulting from decays of new particles in the CMS muon spectrometer also probed new particles with very low masses [28]. The analysis considered a scalar
boson S decaying to a pair of pions, where the scalar boson originates from a Higgs boson
decay. Bounds as low as 10−3 were achieved on the H → SS branching fraction for a range
of scalar boson masses and lifetimes. Related searches with the considered final state have
also been previously conducted by the ATLAS Collaboration [29, 30], targeting prompt as well
as displaced signatures for scenarios with dark photons. Several searches for scalar bosons
produced in rare B-meson decays have been conducted by the LHCb Collaboration [31, 32],
providing constraints on the minimal Higgs portal scenario.
In the search presented in this paper, we aim for unique, complementary sensitivity to the BSM
process depicted in Fig. 1. The data set consists of proton-proton (pp) collisions at a center-ofmass energy of 13 TeV, collected in 2016–2018 by the CMS experiment at the CERN LHC and
corresponding to an integrated luminosity of 138 fb−1 . We fully reconstruct the exclusive final
state, by identifying the collimated hadron (kaon or pion) and muon pairs from the light-scalar
boson decays. By comparing the masses determined from the two track pairs, and requiring
that these are isolated, nearly all of the background is suppressed, yielding sensitivity even for
prompt signals. In addition, there are no SM resonant background contributions, allowing the
mass region below 2 GeV to be probed.

The CMS detector

The CMS apparatus [34, 35] is a multipurpose, nearly hermetic detector, designed to trigger
on [36–38] and identify electrons, muons, photons, and (charged and neutral) hadrons [39–41].
Its central feature is a superconducting solenoid of 6 m internal diameter, providing a magnetic
field of 3.8 T. Within the solenoid volume are a silicon pixel and strip tracker, a lead tungstate
crystal electromagnetic calorimeter (ECAL), and a brass and scintillator hadron calorimeter
(HCAL), each composed of a barrel and two endcap sections. Forward calorimeters extend
the pseudorapidity coverage provided by the barrel and endcap detectors. Muons are reconstructed using gas-ionization detectors interleaved with the layers of the steel return yoke outside the solenoid. More detailed descriptions of the CMS detector, together with a definition of
the coordinate system used and the relevant kinematic variables, can be found in Refs. [34, 35].
The silicon tracker used in 2016 measured charged particles within the pseudorapidity range
|η | < 2.5. For nonisolated particles within |η | < 1.4 and with transverse momentum 1 <
pT < 10 GeV, the track resolutions were typically 1.5% in pT and 25–90 (45–150) µm in the
transverse (longitudinal) impact parameter [41]. At the start of 2017, a new pixel detector was
installed [42]; the upgraded tracker measured particles up to |η | = 3.0 with typical resolutions
of 1.5% in pT and 20–75 µm in the transverse impact parameter [43] for nonisolated particles of
1 < pT < 10 GeV. According to simulation studies [44], similar improvements are expected in
the longitudinal direction.
Events of interest are selected using a two-tiered trigger system. The first level (L1), composed
of custom hardware processors, uses information from the calorimeters and muon detectors to
select events at a rate of around 100 kHz within a fixed latency of 4 µs [36]. The second level,
known as the high-level trigger (HLT), consists of a farm of processors running a version of the
full event reconstruction software optimized for fast processing, and reduces the event rate to
a few kHz before data storage [37, 38].
Muons are measured in the pseudorapidity range |η | < 2.4, with detection planes made using three technologies: drift tubes, cathode strip chambers, and resistive-plate chambers. The
single-muon trigger efficiency exceeds 90% over the full η range, and the efficiency to reconstruct and identify muons is greater than 96%. Matching muons to tracks measured in the
silicon tracker results in a relative transverse momentum resolution, for muons with pT up to
100 GeV, of 1% in the barrel and 3% in the endcaps. The pT resolution in the barrel is better
than 7% for muons with pT up to 1 TeV [40].
A particle-flow algorithm [45] aims to reconstruct and identify each individual particle in an
event, with an optimized combination of information from the various elements of the CMS
detector. The energy of photons is obtained from the ECAL measurement. The energy of an
electron is determined from a combination of the electron momentum at the primary interaction vertex as determined by the tracker, the energy of the corresponding ECAL cluster, and
the energy sum of all bremsstrahlung photons spatially compatible with originating from the
electron track. The energy of a muon is obtained from the curvature of the corresponding track.
The energy of a charged hadron is determined from a combination of the momentum measured
in the tracker and the matching ECAL and HCAL energy deposits, corrected for the response
function of the calorimeters to hadronic showers. Finally, the energy of neutral hadrons is

The primary vertex (PV) is taken to be the vertex corresponding to the hardest scattering in the
event, evaluated using tracking information alone, as described in Section 9.4.1 of Ref. [46].

Simulated samples

The H → SS signal samples are produced at next-to-leading order (NLO) with the POWHEG
2.0 [47–50] Monte Carlo (MC) generator for the dominant Higgs boson production mode of
gluon-gluon fusion. The vector boson fusion and the associated Z and W boson production
modes are also generated at NLO with the same MC generator to account for their contributions
to the total Higgs boson production cross section. With the Higgs boson mass set to 125 GeV,
a range of BSM scalar boson massses is considered between 0.4 and 2 GeV. The proper decay
length of the BSM scalar boson is set to several values between 0.01 and 100 mm (cτgen. = 0.1,
1, 10, 100 mm), to generate signal decays within the tracker detector volume (up to 600 mm
decay length in the laboratory frame). To achieve finer granularity in this range, nearly 40
intermediate signal samples with proper decay length cτint. are obtained by reweighting the
events of the generated signal samples. The reweighting factor is obtained by assuming an
exponential distribution to model the decay vertex of the scalar bosons in both the intermediate
and generated samples.

In the mass range of 0.4 ≤ mS ≤ 2.0 GeV, the hadronic decay mode consists dominantly of
decays to a pair of light hadrons [25]. Selecting events online on the basis of the hadronic-only
decay mode is challenging as triggers based on jets and/or pT sum of hadronic activity typically
have high thresholds. To overcome this limitation, one BSM scalar boson (S 1 ) is fixed to decay
to a pair of muons. The other BSM scalar boson (S 2 ) is set to decay to a pair of charged pions for
mS ≤ 1.0 GeV as the kaon decay mode is not possible because of the limited kinematic phase
space. For mS ≥ 1.1 GeV, the charged-kaon decay mode is dominant according to the studies
in Refs. [12, 25]. Since the charged-pion mode is suppressed for mS ≥ 1.1 GeV, there is little
gain in sensitivity that can be achieved by analyzing it, so S 2 is assumed to decay to charged
kaons only. There is also a nonnegligible contribution coming from the scalar boson decays to
four-pion final states which has not been included in the samples here. The relevant branching
fractions for the muon and charged-hadron decay modes are taken from Ref. [25] and listed
in Table 1. These decays are simulated using the PYTHIA 8.240 [51] generator. The parton
showering, hadronization, and underlying event description are also modeled by PYTHIA 8.240
with the CP2 underlying event tune [52]. The NNPDF3.1 leading order [53] sets of parton
distribution functions are used in the sample generation. The detector response is simulated

The samples are generated separately for each era of the data taking, which includes early 2016,

taking was affected by saturation effects in the silicon strip tracker, which were fixed for the
late 2016 data taking [55].

Event selection

The large mass difference between the Higgs boson and the BSM scalar bosons leads to their
large Lorentz boosts and highly collimated decays. This typically results in at least one of the
muons being produced with high transverse momentum √
(⟨ pT ⟩ ≥ 40 GeV) and small angular
distance ∆R between the two muons (defined by ∆R = (∆η )2 + (∆ϕ)2 , where ϕ is the azimuthal angle). An isolated single-muon trigger with a pT threshold of 24 (27) GeV has been
employed to collect the data in the 2016 and 2018 (2017) data sets. For the BSM scalar bosons
with proper decay lengths ≤1 mm, the trigger provides a signal efficiency of ∼80%. In spite of
the optimal pT threshold, the presence of a subleading muon track biases the isolation around
the leading muon and leads to a slightly reduced signal efficiency. For larger cτ values (10–
100 mm), the signal efficiency drops to 25–50% because the single-muon trigger is designed
to select prompt muons and reject events with muons produced with large displacements. In
the 2018 data set, corresponding to an integrated luminosity of 60 fb−1 , the efficiency for the
displaced signatures is improved by the inclusion of displaced dimuon triggers that require at
least two muons reconstructed in the muon detector only [40] with pT > 23 GeV each. These
triggers do not impose a PV constraint or isolation requirements on the muon tracks, improving the sensitivity to both prompt and displaced signatures. This leads to an increase in the
signal efficiency of ∼10%, limited only by the larger pT threshold on the subleading muon. For
the 2016 and 2017 data sets, dimuon triggers (prompt and displaced) were also considered.
However, these triggers show a low signal efficiency because of the isolation requirements on
the subleading muon, the mass constraints on the dimuon system, and the high pT thresholds
for the muons, so they are not used in the analysis.
At least two muons reconstructed offline and two charged hadrons h ± are required in each
event. The muon collection consists of particles reconstructed as such by the PF algorithm,
either from a global fit across each subdetector or as a ”tracker muon” for which the tracker
track matches at least one muon segment after extrapolation. Additional requirements on the
muons include pT > 5 GeV and |η | < 2.4. To ensure that the selected events are well above
the trigger threshold, the leading muon is required to have a pT > 26 (29) GeV, for the 2016
and 2018 (2017) data set. For the events in the 2018 data set that are triggered by the displaced
dimuon triggers, at least two muons with pT > 26 GeV are required. The charged hadrons are

In order to reconstruct the BSM scalar boson candidates, oppositely charged muon pairs are
formed with a distance of ∆R(µµ ) < 0.4. For each pair, a vertex fit is carried out using a Kalman
vertex filtering algorithm [41]. A valid vertex fit is produced when the fitted position lies within
the tracker volume and the fit converges within a set maximum number of iterations. Among
the pairs with a valid fitted vertex, the highest pT pair is selected as the pair of signal muons
coming from the BSM scalar boson. Additionally, the muons are then refitted to the dimuon
decay vertex to improve the pT resolution and the overall mass resolution of the reconstructed
BSM scalar boson. For identification of the charged-hadron decay vertex, a similar procedure is
followed with a vertex fit of oppositely charged π ± /K ± pairs. The highest pT pair is retained
as the signal candidate. The hadronic tracks are then refitted to the dihadron decay vertex.
To suppress the presence of vertices arising from low-energy pileup, events with the pT of the
dihadron candidate less than 20 GeV are rejected. The dimuon and dihadron candidates are
additionally required to pass a loose mass selection of both mµµ and mhh to be less than 5 GeV.
The Higgs boson candidate mass, mµµhh , is reconstructed as the invariant mass of the four
objects (µµhh). Events in the mass range 110 < mµµhh < 140 GeV are selected. The signal
region (SR) is defined as 122.5 < mµµhh < 127.5 GeV while the sidebands of the distribution,
110 < mµµhh < 122.5 GeV and 127.5 < mµµhh < 140 GeV, are used to define the control region
(CR) for background prediction. The criteria described so far form the baseline selection.
Comparing SM simulations and data in the CR, the dominant background is composed of
multijet events from QCD processes, followed by a contribution from tt production. The QCD
multijet background is dominated by events with heavy-flavor jets, which can yield muon pairs
in the jet hadronization. Additional selection criteria are applied to suppress the contribution
coming from these background processes and are explained below.
An additional requirement is placed on the dimuon and dihadron invariant masses. The BSM
scalar boson is weakly coupled to the SM and is expected to have a small width compared to
the detector resolution. Taking into account the detector resolution and final-state radiation for
the muons and the hadrons, the signal dimuon and dihadron invariant masses are localized in a
narrow region around the BSM scalar boson mass. To exploit this feature, a selection is defined
in the two-dimensional (2D) plane of mµµ and mhh using all the BSM scalar boson masses considered. This is determined by fitting the dimuon and dihadron invariant mass distributions
separately for the prompt-like (i.e., cτ = 0.1 mm) signal samples with a Gaussian function on
the core of the distribution to reduce the impact of material interactions and reconstruction
failures that lead to the non-Gaussian tails. To ensure a high signal efficiency, the fitted mean
(µfit ) and fitted resolution σfit of the signal dimuon and dihadron mass distributions are used
to construct µfit ± 2.5σfit bounds separately. In Fig. 2 (left), the 2D mass plane of mµµ and mhh
is presented for a BSM scalar boson signal with mS = 0.6 GeV and cτ = 0.1 mm, after applying
the baseline selection. The extracted bounds for the various signal masses considered are overlaid as red boxes. The edges of the boxes are then linearly interpolated to form the 2D diagonal
mass selection that accepts all events falling in it. This selection criterion is applied on all displaced signatures as well. Figure 2 (right) shows the distribution of the expected background
estimated from the CR for the 2016–2018 data set, assuming decays to charged pions, where
the lines denote the 2D diagonal mass selection.

Systematic uncertainties

The background prediction uncertainty is dominated by the statistical uncertainty from the limited number of events in the CR. Alternate functional forms to the exponential model, including a power law and linear functions, were explored. The variation in the resulting background
predictions, propagated via the transfer factor f SR/CR , was found to be very small compared to
the statistical uncertainty, and was neglected. In the merged non-prompt category, additional
sources of uncertainties are included to account for the multiple transfer factors. The uncertainty in f 1 is up to 100% in the displaced category and arises from the nearly zero events in the
sideband after applying the event selection. The transfer factor f 2 has an associated uncertainty
in a range of 10–35%.

Several experimental and theoretical uncertainties affect the signal prediction. The leading
theoretical uncertainty, which dominates the total signal uncertainty, comes from the change in
the Higgs boson pT distribution arising from renormalization and factorization scale variations.
This uncertainty is estimated by varying the renormalization and factorization scales up and
down by a factor of two, excluding the two extreme variations, and amounts to ≈17%.
The leading experimental uncertainties come from the corrections applied to the secondary vertex reconstruction of displaced vertices and the isolation requirements applied on the hadrons.
Corrections to the displaced-vertex reconstruction efficiency are derived as a function of displacement and momentum by using decays of neutral kaons to pairs of charged pions in data,
based on the methodology of Ref. [57]. The uncertainty ranges between 5–40%, with the larger
one present for the non-prompt categories. A similar methodology is adopted to derive the uncertainties in the signal mass window constructed for the event selection. These corrections
and uncertainties are applied to both the dimuon and the dihadron vertices. A conservative estimate of the uncertainty for the muon and hadron isolation requirements is applied. This uncertainty is calculated separately for the muon and hadron isolation thresholds. In the prompt
category, the uncertainty associated with the muon (hadron) isolation is 5–10 (5–15)%. In the
non-prompt categories, the isolation requirements are much looser, leading to a highly efficient
selection, and the uncertainty in the muon (hadron) isolation leads to a 2.5 (10)% uncertainty in
the signal yield. The uncertainty in the reconstruction of hadron tracks is ∼5% [58]. Uncertainties in the muon identification and reconstruction of close-by muons in the tracker amounts to
less than 2% per muon [27]. Additionally, a 5% uncertainty per muon is assigned to account
for the reconstruction of displaced muons [59].
Corrections to the trigger efficiency in simulation are derived with a ”tag-and-probe” method [60]
using the J/ψ resonance, which closely emulate the signal features. The related trigger uncertainty is found to be 5–13%. The pileup modeling uncertainties amount to 1%. The integrated
luminosities for the 2016, 2017, and 2018 data-taking years have 1–3% respective uncertainties [61–63], with an overall uncertainty of 1.6%. Finally, the uncertainty due to the size of the
signal samples is also included and varies from 2 to 15%, with larger values towards the larger
cτ hypotheses. The uncertainties are summarized in Table 4.

Results

With the application of the selection and the related uncertainties, the predicted background
count is used to perform a statistical interpretation of the results. Under the background-only
hypothesis, 95% confidence level (CL) upper limits on the branching fraction of B(H → SS )
are calculated using the CLs method [64, 65] for the benchmark model. The contributions from
Higgs boson via vector boson fusion and production in association with a vector boson are
evaluated and accounted for as well, and increase the signal yields (across different mS and
decay lengths) by about 9.1%. The limits are derived using a modified frequentist approach,
with the likelihood ratio as the test statistic. The results are determined with the CMS statistical
analysis tool C OMBINE [66] which is based on the R OO F IT [67] and R OO S TATS [68] frameworks.
Figures 7 and 8 show the observed and expected limits as functions of the decay length for different mS hypotheses. In Fig. 7, the limits for mS = 0.6 and 1 GeV are presented, using the final
state of two muons and two charged pions. Below cτ ≲ 0.1 mm, the limits start to plateau with
respect to the decay length. Most of the events in this region fall within the prompt category,
which benefits from efficient reconstruction and triggering capabilities. This also indicates that
below a certain decay length, the events approach the prompt-like signature and the difference in the reconstructed vertex displacement between the decay lengths is negligible. Beyond
cτ = 0.1 mm, the limits start to worsen as the vertices are more displaced, and the events are
harder to trigger on and reconstruct. Similar behavior is observed in Fig. 8, where the limits for
mS = 1.1 and 1.6 GeV are presented, using the final state of two muons and two charged kaons.
The observed limits over the full mass range of 0.4–2 GeV are shown in Fig. 9 as functions of
mS and cτ. Improved sensitivity is achieved at higher masses because of lower backgrounds,
while a drop near 1 GeV is observed resulting from a smaller B(S → µµ ). For all other mass
hypotheses considered, a limit on B(H → SS ) of the order of 10−3 (10−4 ) for proper decay
lengths up to cτ ∼ 10 mm (1 mm) is achieved.

Summary

An exclusive search for light exotic scalar
√ bosons has been presented using proton-proton collision data collected in 2016–2018 at s = 13 TeV, and corresponding to an integrated luminosity of 138 fb−1 . The analysis accesses a unique hadronic decay mode where the low mass
of the scalar boson restricts hadronization and dominantly allows decays to only a pair of light
hadrons. The Higgs boson-mediated production of a pair of light scalar bosons decaying within
the CMS trackers is sought in the final state of two muons and two charged hadrons. Scalar boson masses below 2 GeV with a proper decay length cτ up to 100 mm are probed. The boosted
topology, the isolation, and mass constraints between the reconstructed dimuon and dihadron
resonances are used to reduce the background. Considering the benchmark signal model [25],
this search excludes at 95% confidence level branching fractions of the Higgs boson to light
scalar bosons greater than 10−4 and covers a largely unexplored parameter space of light longlived particles.

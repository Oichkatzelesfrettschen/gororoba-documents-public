We establish the average-case hardness of Betti number estimation on random
clique complexes via a reduction from the planted clique problem. We further
show that our reduction implies a series of hardness results for many problems in
both classical and quantum Topological Data Analysis (qTDA). Under the classical
planted clique conjecture, no randomized polynomial-time Betti number estimator
achieves additive error below 12 with constant advantage. Under a new quantum
planted clique conjecture that we introduce, the same conclusion holds for quantum
polynomial-time algorithms. We also obtain related conditional hardness results for
homology vanishing, additive approximations with larger error tolerances, preparation of simplex and harmonic states, cycle recovery, and counting eigenvalues at low
energy. Our reduction clarifies the structural requirements for quantum advantage
in TDA and provides a new lens to investigate the classical and quantum complexity
of related problems.

1 Introduction
Topological Data Analysis is a framework for studying the shape of complex data in high
dimensions. One of its basic invariants is the Betti number β d , which is the dimension of the
d-th homology group and counts independent homology classes in dimension d. TDA has been
applied to biological data [TZH15], voting data [LSL+ 13], and financial time series [dJFNCR25].
It has also been used in cancer research [NLC11, RMR+ 20] and medical imaging [SFH+ 23]. For
large complexes, constructing and reducing the relevant boundary matrices can be computationally expensive. This has motivated a long line of work on quantum algorithms for Betti
number estimation. See Ref. [GK19] for an early review. Complexity results have also identified
limitations of quantum algorithms for related topological problems [EP14, SL23]. These worstcase results do not, however, determine the complexity of instances that may be drawn from a
specified distribution. In this work, we study the classical and quantum average-case hardness
of Betti number estimation under the distribution associated with the planted clique problem.
Quantum algorithms for computing Betti numbers. Lloyd, Garnerone, and Zanardi (LGZ)
[LGZ16] were the first to propose a quantum algorithm that estimates the normalized Betti
number β d / f d to inverse-polynomial additive error. A decade of follow-up work has both
built this sketch into a genuine pipeline and, simultaneously optimised the algorithms. On the
constructive side, these works have given rigorous analyses of the spectral gap and density
dependencies [GK19, GCD22], extension to persistent Betti numbers [Hay22], qubit- and depthefficient implementations [UAS+ 21, MGB22, AUC+ 22], and end-to-end resource estimates for
candidate advantage regimes [BSG+ 24]. On the limiting side, the efficiency guarantee has been
understood to rest on three promises:
1. the complex must be clique-dense (else the simplex state cannot be prepared efficiently);
2. the spectral gap of ∆d must be at least inverse polynomial;
3. and the normalized Betti number must not be exponentially small.
Path-integral and Krylov-type classical algorithms estimate normalized Betti numbers under
closely related density and gap assumptions [AGSS22, BSG+ 24], eroding the speedup exactly
where the quantum algorithm is efficient. Furthermore, Schmidhuber and Lloyd observed
that generic complexes have normalized Betti numbers so small that the estimate carries no
information precisely where state preparation is easiest [SL23].
The worst-case complexity landscape. In parallel, a sequence of hardness results has outlined the intrinsic difficulty of problems in qTDA. Computing Betti numbers of a clique
complex exactly is #P-hard, and approximating them, even to within a multiplicative factor is NP-hard [SL23]. Computing the Euler characteristic (an alternating sum of the number
of faces (elements) of dimensions i in the complex) is already #P-hard for general abstract
complexes [RSdC13]. Deciding whether the homology of a clique complex vanishes is NPhard [AS16]. Cade and Crichigno established this for the closely related cohomology problem
of supersymmetric systems [CC24], Crichigno and Kohler for clique complexes [CK24], and
King and Kohler for the gapped (promise) version on weighted graphs, which they also place
inside QMA [KK23]. For the estimation task the sharpest calibration is due to Gyurik, Cade,
and Dunjko [GCD22]: estimating the low-lying spectral density of a succinctly specified sparse
Hermitian matrix is DQC1 -hard, so an efficient classical simulation of the quantum estimation
pipeline on all inputs would collapse the one-clean-qubit model. On the related problem of
homology localization, Chen and Freedman proved that computing minimal cycle representatives
of homology classes is NP-hard to approximate [CF11].

All of these results are worst-case: the hard instances are adversarial, and none of them
addresses the complexity of Betti-number estimation on the inputs TDA is typically run on. For
instance, the theory of random topology shows that clique complexes of Erdős–Rényi graphs
have highly structured homology: for X ( G (n, p)) for a fixed 0 < p < 1 the nontrivial homology
statistically concentrates in a narrow band of dimensions and vanishes outside it [Kah09, Kah14],
so the measure of the set of worst-case instances may simply be extremely close to zero on
random inputs. Whether any natural distribution over inputs makes Betti-number estimation
hard on average for classical and quantum algorithms alike has, to our knowledge, remained
open. It is this gap that we address in this paper.
Average-case hardness from planted clique. Our source of average-case hardness is the
planted clique problem, introduced by Jerrum [Jer92] and Kučera [Kuč95]: distinguish an Erdős–
Rényi random graph G (n, 12 ) from the same graph with a clique planted on k random vertices
that we call G (n, 12 , k ). Spectral and combinatorial algorithms succeed in polynomial time
√
for k = Ω( n) [AKS98, DGGP14, DM15], while for (2 + ε) log2 n ≤ k ≤ n1/2−ε no efficient
algorithm is known despite three decades of effort. The conjecture that none exists is supported by unconditional lower bounds in every standard restricted model: statistical-query algorithms [FGR+ 17], the sum-of-squares hierarchy [BHK+ 19], Lovász–Schrijver relaxations [FK03],
bounded-depth circuits [Ros08], the Metropolis process [Jer92], and low-degree polynomial
tests [KWB19, SW22]. On this basis, the planted clique conjecture (PC) has become a standard hardness primitive for average-case reductions, underlying lower bounds for sparse
PCA [BR13], submatrix detection [MW15], community detection [HWX15], certification of
restricted isometry [KZ14], Nash-equilibrium approximation [HK11], and a web of further
problems [BBH18, BB20, MRS21]. Recently, Hirahara and Shimizu proved that many formalizations of PC are essentially equivalent [HS24]. Their equivalences supply two ingredients
we use throughout this work. First, hardness at every constant distinguishing advantage is
equivalent to the standard conjecture, which lets us state our theorem for algorithms succeeding with probability 12 + δ for arbitrarily small constant δ > 0. Second, their results calibrate
exactly how far such statements can be pushed: a folklore edge-counting test distinguishes
the two distributions with advantage Θ(k2 /n) [Tre18, HS24], so no reduction can rule out all
inverse-polynomial advantage, a subtlety that has invalidated at least one published hardness
framework [ERSY22] as noted by [HS24]. Therefore, our constant-advantage statement for
(q)TDA is also optimal in this sense. In [HS24] the authors further identify a detection–recovery
gap [HS24, BJ23, SW22]: while detection admits the Θ(k2 /n)-advantage edge count, recovering
the clique is hard under PC conjecture even with success probability n−c for every constant
c, a distinction our corollaries inherit. Figure 1 depicts all implications we have identified as
corollaries of our main result. In particular, state preparation for the LGZ algorithm, homology
localization, vanishing homology, Betti number to large additive approximation, are all hard on
average under the distribution Dk originating from Erdős–Rényi clique complexes.
The importance of the distribution choice. Average-case hardness is inherently defined
over some fixed choice of a distribution D ( x ) of problem instances x. Different choices of D ( x )
might lead to different average-case hardness statements, thus a choice of D ( x ) should be well
motivated. In this work we adopt the distribution Dk = 12 G (n, 12 ) + 12 G (n, 12 , k ) from the
planted clique problem, which naturally derives from the Erdős–Rényi distribution G (n, 12 ) (see
sec 3.1 for definitions). Erdős–Rényi distribution is a very common and well studied distribution
of random complexes [Kah09, Kah14, SNK18, SRCF+ 19] which has been shown to be relevant
to modeling the human brain [SRCF+ 19], as well as to machine learning and computer vision
tasks such as object matching, object detection, and Structure from Motion (SfM) [SNK18]. We
use the balanced distribution for the planted clique problem. This choice is useful because
planted clique is widely used in average-case reductions and because our reduction leaves the

A new lens on quantum advantage in TDA. Our results carry implications in two directions
for the prospect of quantum advantage. Negatively, they close off one tempting argument:
Betti number estimation and other aforementioned qTDA tasks remain hard on natural random
inputs, so under this distribution one cannot witness a quantum speedup and moreover, at
the critical dimension, even the pipeline’s state-preparation step is infeasible, for reasons
independent of the well-known density obstruction [GCD22, SL23, BSG+ 24]. Positively, they
sharpen the search: any demonstration of quantum advantage in TDA must locate instance
families that evade our reduction. For example, complexes that are clique-dense with inversepolynomially gapped Laplacians and large normalized Betti numbers and remain classically
hard for reasons other than the planted clique structure. We show that under the distribution
of instances coming from the planted clique problem the problem of Betti number estimation
is hard for both classical and quantum algorithms. The reduction thus functions as a filter
on proposed advantage regimes, and clarifies the boundary between the worst-case quantum
hardness results [CC24, CK24, KK23, GCD22] and the average-case landscape closer to the
practical data on which TDA is expected to operate.

1.1 Organisation
The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 introduces our main theorem, the
average-case hardness of Betti-number estimation on clique complexes drawn from the graph
distribution of the planted clique problem. Section 3 fixes notation and recalls the definitions
of planted-clique problem, clique complexes and Betti numbers, and the local access model
through which the graph specifies the complex and its combinatorial Laplacian in polynomial
time. Section 4 gives the reduction from planted clique to Betti-number estimation, proves
Theorem 2.1, and discusses how the argument behaves for skeleta of other dimensions and
random graphs with varying parameter p. Section 5 draws out the algorithmic consequences.
Section 6 outlines how our contribution helps with the search for quantum advantage in qTDA.
Section 7 derives the corollaries for other tasks of quantum Topological Data Analysis, namely
normalized Betti number estimation, homology vanishing, homology localization, simplexand harmonic-state preparation and the low-lying spectral density, and compares them with
worst-case state-preparation hardness. Section 8 closes with a discussion and a list of open
problems.

2 Contributions
Our results connect the hardness of the well known problem in graph theory, namely the planted
clique problem, to a family of problems in computational algebraic topology, known broadly
under the name of topological data analysis. We give the first conditional average-case hardness
reduction for problems in topological data analysis. Our work provides a new framework that
allows one to investigate questions in algebraic topology via graph theoretical tools. Based
on the established classical planted clique conjecture [HS24, Kuč95, Jer92] we derive the classical
average-case hardness results. We further introduce the quantum planted clique conjecture (see 2),
based on which we derive the quantum conditional average-case hardness of Betti number
estimation (and other problems) of clique complexes drawn from the planted clique distribution
Dk = 12 G (n, 12 ) + 12 G (n, 12 , k). A demonstration of an exponential quantum speedup for the Betti
number estimation under Dk would not only falsify the quantum planted clique conjecture
but also imply the first average-case quantum speedup (assuming the classical planted clique
conjecture stands).

2.1 Technical overview
We give a simple reduction from planted clique problem to the Betti-number estimation on clique
complexes. The starting observation is that the clique complex of a complete graph on k
vertices is a k − 1 simplex σk−1 , which is contractible, so planting a k-clique contributes no

2.2 Classical and quantum results
We highlight that the reduction presented in section 4 is the same both classically and in the
quantum setting. Under the well established classical planted clique conjecture (Assumption 1)
Betti number estimation and other problems mentioned in section 7 are classically average-case
hard. We introduce a quantum clique conjecture (assumption 2) under which the conditional
average-case hardness follows in the quantum setting. A polynomial time quantum algorithm
for Betti number estimation in the hard regime would render the conjecture false and the
problems in section 7 quantum-average-case easy, while remaining classical average-case hard.
This remains an open problem.

3.2 Random graphs and the planted-clique problem
G (n, 12 ) denotes the Erdős–Rényi graph on vertex set [n] = {1, . . . , n} in which each of the (n2 )
edges is present independently with probability 12 . The planted distribution G (n, 12 , k ) is obtained
by drawing G0 ∼ G (n, 12 ), choosing a uniformly random k-subset S ⊆ [n], and adding every
edge within S.
Definition 1 (Planted-clique detection). PC LIQUE (n, k ) is the task of deciding, given a random
graph G drawn from G (n, 12 ) (the null case) whether or not it has been modified by planting a
k-clique resulting in G (n, 12 , k ) (the planted case). In other words, the problem is to decide which
case it is (whether the graph comes from the distribution G (n, 12 ) or not).
The largest clique size of G (n, 12 ) is Cmax ( G (n, 12 )) = (2 + o (1)) log2√n w.h.p. For k < (2 −
ε) log2 n the plant is information-theoretically undetectable; for k ≳ n spectral algorithms
detect it in polynomial time. The intermediate window is believed intractable.

The average-case planted clique hardness holds for every constant value of parameter p [HS24].

5 Algorithmic implications
Because we have established the hardness of Betti number estimation on random clique complexes, it is natural to ask which of the ingredients that make quantum algorithms efficient are
present, and which are not. We organise this section around the three promises under which
the LGZ quantum algorithm is efficient, recalled from the introduction:
(P1) the complex is clique-dense, so the uniform simplex state |ψd ⟩ ∝ ∑σ∈Xd (G) |σ⟩ can be
prepared in polynomial time;
(P2) the combinatorial Laplacian ∆d has an at least polynomially small spectral gap, so 1/gap =
poly(n);
(P3) the normalized Betti number β d / f d is not exponentially small.
An advantage requires a family that is quantumly easy while classically hard, hence, by the
previous paragraphs and by [AGSS22], a family on which (P1), (P2) and (P3) hold simultaneously at a growing dimension, since at bounded constant dimension the classical estimator
already matches the quantum one. The Erdős–Rényi model cannot supply this: at growing dimension its clique density is small, (P1) fails (see proof of 4.1), and neither algorithm is efficient.
Any candidate family must therefore lie outside the distribution Dk , and it must in addition be
classically hard for a reason other than a hidden planted clique, because the planted structure,
we have shown, is equally opaque to quantum algorithms. Concretely, a candidate family
should satisfy the promises P1,P2,P3 and be classically hard. The gapped clique-homology

6.3 Region for average-case quantum-advantage
Our results, together with the classical algorithm of [AGSS22] and the efficient state preparation
problem[GCD22, SL23], partition the (d, α) landscape of Erdős–Rényi clique complexes into
three regions, and by elimination we arrive at a single candidate for average-case quantum
advantage.
• PC-hard region ((2 + ϵ) log n < d < n1/2−ϵ ). The null complex is empty w.h.p, β d = 0,
and the planted clique creates the only homology. This is where Theorem 2.1 rules out
average-case advantage: Betti estimation is PC-hard, but it is hard for both classical
and quantum algorithms and, by Schmidhuber–Lloyd [SL23], the invariant carries no
information on typical inputs. Thus no quantum advantage.
• Bounded dimension (d = O(1)). The window is nonempty here only for α = Θ(1), and
there the normalized Betti number is estimated in polynomial time classically [AGSS22].
Any quantum algorithm is matched by a classical one. Thus quantum advantage is ruled
out.
• The window at growing dimension ( d+1 1 < α < 1d , d → ∞). Here the normalized Betti
number is near-maximal (P3), the gap can be favourable (P2), and, by 6.4, the plantedclique reduction provably fails. One remaining obstacle is (P1) which causes the quantum
state preparation step to be inefficient with known techniques. Nevertheless, the main
observation is that the average-case hardness we establish for the problem does not reach
into this region. This is the interesting and non-trivial regime our reduction does not affect
and the question of average-case quantum advantage remains open and plausible.

7 Implications
In this section we present the corollaries of our reduction from section 4. We work with the
quantum hardness assumptions for planted clique (assumptions 2, 3), but all corollaries hold
for classical computation if the quantum conjectures are replaced with the more established
classical hardness assumptions.
We introduce the following quantum version of the strong search PC conjecture, for the
classical version we refer the reader to [HS24]. This will be of use for some of the corollaries.
Assumption 3 (Quantum version of the strong search conjecture [HS24]). For any constants
α ∈ (0, 1/2) and c > 0, any (classical randomized [HS24]) quantum polynomial-time algorithm
fails to find a k-clique in G (n, 12 , k ) with probability 1 − n−c (the probability of success is inverse
polynomial) for all sufficiently large n and k ≤ n1/2−α .
Usually the worst-case complexity results refer to a random clique complex X ( G ); averagecase results from this work are about the d-skeleton of a clique complex from distribution Dk in
the same local-access model.

7.3 Other implications
In this section we mention other implications of the findings presented in this work.
7.3.1 Restricted models (unconditional).
Because the reduction is direct and the problem instance remains on the same graph, the
unconditional planted-clique lower bounds transfer to every estimation problem above with no
conjecture. Concretely: no low-degree polynomial test [KWB19, SW22] and no statistical-query
algorithm [FGR+ 17] distinguishes the null and planted values of β d in the PC window; and by
the sum-of-squares lower bound for planted clique [BHK+ 19], no polynomial-size, constantdegree sum-of-squares certificate proves Hd (skeld X ( G )) = 0 for G ∼ G (n, 12 ), even though this
vanishing holds w.h.p.
7.3.2 Achieving quantum advantage
The ongoing search for quantum advantage in quantum Topological Data Analysis has resulted
in the number of complexity results which rule out specific families of problems. In this
work we show that both classical and quantumly the Betti number estimation problem as
well as all the problems described in section 7 are hard on average under the widely believed
planted clique conjecture. We have identified a region where our reduction does not reach,
leaving the average-case hardness question open. Moreover the identified region exhibits
non-trivial homology, large spectral gaps, but still suffers from the state-preparation problem
(P1), rendering existing quantum algorithms inefficient. However, for instances in this region if
one identifies perhaps instance specific efficient techniques for state-preparation then one might
be capable of demonstrating instance- or family-specific quantum advantage.

8.1 Extensions of the average-case hardness framework
1. Average-case complexity under other natural distributions. Determine the average-case
complexity of Betti-number estimation under probability distributions other than the
balanced planted-clique distribution considered here.
Even for pure Erdős–Rényi flag complexes, it remains necessary to distinguish probabilistic
concentration of the invariant from the computational complexity of estimating it at a
specified precision.
2. Converse reductions and distributional completeness. Determine whether planted clique
can be recovered from a suitable Betti-number oracle in both directions, or whether Bettinumber estimation is complete for a natural class of planted distributional problems. More
generally, identify transformations between hidden subgraph problems and topological
estimation problems that preserve the input distribution, success probability, and relevant
access model.
3. Persistent and multiparameter extensions. Extend our reduction to persistent homology
and multiparameter persistence. A natural starting point is the skeletal filtration

persistence, cokernel persistence, and selected multiparameter Hilbert-function queries.
This would complement existing quantum algorithms for persistent Betti numbers [Hay22].

8.2 Biological applications
1. Online and zigzag qTDA for evolving tissues. Develop quantum algorithms for timedependent sequences
K0 ←→ K1 ←→ · · · ←→ KT ,
in which simplices can both appear and disappear. An important question is whether a
simplex state or harmonic state prepared at time t can be used as a warm start at time t + 1,
leading to an amortized advantage over repeated classical matrix reduction. This has a
number of practical biological applications which include tumour evolution, immune-cell
migration, and vascular remodelling.
2. Directed topology of biological flow and signalling. Develop qTDA methods for directed
biological networks, including signalling pathways, gene-regulatory networks, metabolic
networks, vascular flow, neuronal circuits, and polarized molecular interactions.
3. Higher-order topology in multi-omics and cellular interactions. Construct biologically
motivated simplicial or hypergraph models in which a higher-dimensional simplex represents a genuine joint interaction rather than only the clique completion of pairwise edges.
Examples include multi-cell signalling events, protein complexes, gene-regulatory modules, microbiome communities and beyond. One should determine whether higher-order
topological features improve prediction beyond graph-based models and whether the
corresponding simplices can be sampled without enumerating the full complex.

8.3 Quantum advantage
1. Common access models for classical and quantum algorithms. Compare quantum algorithms with the corresponding classical algorithms under the same input assumptions.
The comparison should include exact sparse boundary reduction, path-integral estimators,
Krylov and Chebyshev methods, stochastic Lanczos quadrature, and application-specific
sampling algorithms [AGSS22, BSG+ 24].
2. Amortized advantage. Many applications rely on performing the same topological computation over many scales, time points, parameter values, candidate molecules, or bootstrap
samples. Determine whether quantum states, block encodings, or spectral estimates can be
reused across related instances. An amortized advantage over a long sequence of correlated
queries may be achievable even when no advantage is present for a single isolated complex.

8.4 Cryptography
1. Betti-number estimation as a proof-of-work problem. Determine whether the averagecase hardness of Betti-number estimation can be combined with an efficient verification
mechanism.

1.1

The classical Theory

The first and second fundamental theorems of invariant theory appear in
H. Weyl’s book The classical groups [25] as a basic tool to understand the
representation Theory of classical groups. One remarkable feature of these
Theorems is that they are ubiquitous, they appear classically in at least 3
different and apparently unrelated forms (see the next section).

The theorems were first developed in characteristic 0 and then extended,
with considerable effort, to all characteristics, see [9], [11], [26], [10].
In this paper we present yet another form of these theorems and will
restrict to characteristic 0 (but see the very last comment). We will also
develop the theory only for the general linear group GL(V ), that is the
group of all linear transformations of a vector space V finite dimensional
over a field F (which one can assume to be Q).
For the other classical groups similar results hold (one may for instance
approach them as in [16]) and will be treated elsewhere.

2.1

Algebras with trace

Let us quickly recall this formalism which will be used throughout this paper.
Definition 2.2. An algebra with trace is an associative algebra A over some
ring F together with a linear map tr : A → A satisfying the following axioms,
see [2] Chapter 2.3 or [19].
tr(a) · b = b · tr(a), tr(a · b) = tr(b · a), tr(tr(a) · b) = tr(a) · tr(b), ∀a, b ∈ A.
Then the image tr(A) of tr is a central subalgebra, called the trace algebra
and tr is tr(A) linear.
Remark that in the definition, in the approach of universal algebra, we
do not assume that trace takes values in F .
Algebras with trace form a category where maps are trace compatible
homomorphisms.
Algebras with trace admit free algebras.
Definition 2.3. The free algebra with trace in some list of variables X is
T ⟨X⟩ := F ⟨X⟩[tr(M )]. That is the polynomial algebra in the elements tr(M )
over the usual free algebra F ⟨X⟩.
Its trace algebra is the polynomial algebra F [tr(M )] in the elements
tr(M ) over F . By tr(M ) we denote the class of a monomial M up to cyclic
equivalence, (cf. [19] for a detailed definition).

Tensor Polynomials

This paper can be also used as an introduction to the study of polynomial
maps f : Mdk → Md⊗n which are equivariant under the conjugation action of GL(Q, d). Or with intrinsic notations f : End(V )k → End(V )⊗n =
End(V ⊗n ). These maps form a non commutative algebra using the algebra

(i)
tensor variables xj := 1⊗i−1 ⊗ xj ⊗ 1⊗n−i , where xj is a matrix variable.
(i)

Definition 2.7. We call the xj tensor variables and the (non commutative)
polynomials they generate tensor polynomials.
The concept of tensor polynomial or of tensor trace identities as far
as I know was not considered by algebraists. I wish to thank Felix Huber
for pointing out this notion which seems to play some role in Quantum
Information Theory, see [12] and [23].
Given a free algebra F ⟨X⟩ and an associative algebra A over a field F ,
a map f : X → A induces a homomorphism f : F ⟨X⟩ → A and conversely
a homomorphism f : F ⟨X⟩ → A is determined by its values on X.
The polynomial identities of A are the elements of F ⟨X⟩ vanishing under
all evaluations.
Now though, such a map f defines also, for all integers n, a homomorphism of the corresponding n–fold tensor products:
f ⊗n : F ⟨X⟩⊗n → A⊗n .
One can thus define as n–fold tensor identity for A an element G ∈ F ⟨X⟩⊗n
vanishing for all evaluations in A, i.e. f ⊗n (G) = 0, ∀f : X → A.
A similar notion holds for A an algebra with trace.

Universal algebra is a concept first introduced by Garrett Birkhoff, see [5]
and P. Chon [8], or [7] for a more extensive treatment. If one has a class of

in turn are determined by substitution maps X → F(X). T –ideals appear
naturally as ideals of identities of algebras in the given class of algebras.
In this paper we need a small generalization of this notion to take care
of the tensor structure, see Definition 3.6 and 3.17.

As for tensor trace identities we know that for n = 1 they are generated

For higher n the situation is more complex since the algebra of equivariant maps is not flat over its trace algebra except for the special case
n = |X| = 2. This is actually interesting and partially treated in [21]. Let
us explain what happens in general.
We have a map j : T ⟨X⟩⊗n → Qd ⟨Ξ⟩⊗n . A simple argument shows that
its Kernel is formed by the tensor trace identities since we may view this as a
specialization to generic matrices. Now when we use the d–Cayley Hamilton
identity this map factors through a map jd : Td ⟨X⟩⊗n → Qd ⟨Ξ⟩⊗n .
By simple localization arguments we then see that if a ∈ Td ⟨X⟩⊗n is in
the Kernel of jd then there is an invariant b so that ba = 0. In fact if b is
any central polynomial we have bk a = 0 for some k.
We can also take any nonzero discriminant δ := det(tr(bi bj )) of d2 elements bi ∈ Td ⟨X⟩. Then after localizing Td ⟨X⟩[δ−1 ] is a free module with

So we may say that up to multiplication by some power of this discriminant a tensor trace identity can be deduced from the d–Cayley Hamilton
identity.
On the other hand the situation is similar to that of functional identities,

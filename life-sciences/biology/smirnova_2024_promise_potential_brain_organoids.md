2. Generating Brain Organoids
Brain organoids are mostly derived from human iPSCs, which
are generated by reprogramming adult somatic cells into a
pluripotent state akin to embryonic stem cells.[9] This allows the
generation of stem cells from individual patients, capturing their
unique genetic background. iPSCs can self-renew indeﬁnitely—
or at least for long time considering genetic instabilities— in culture while maintaining the potential to diﬀerentiate into all cell
types of the body. However, a number of variations have been described for in our case the resulting brain organoids (Figure 1).
The seminal protocol for generating brain organoids was published by Lancaster et al. in 2013.[2] It leverages the intrinsic

organoids. Their interior regions become hypoxic, nutrientdeprived, and accumulate waste products,[12] which restricts
organoid growth and survival. The lack of sensory input and
functional neuronal connectivity limits complex neural activity.
Still, organoids strikingly mimic human brain development in
vitro,[19] and have proven broadly useful across neuroscience
ﬁelds.
The term “brainspheres” has been used somewhat interchangeably with brain organoids. More recently, brainspheres
or cortical spheroids are terms used to describe models which
are simpler and more speciﬁc in their representation. They primarily resemble the developing in vivo human prenatal brain
and are particularly useful for studying the cortex. Unlike brain
organoids, brainspheres do not typically represent multiple brain
regions (Table 1).

3. Applications in Toxicology and Safety Testing
A major application out of many (Figure 1) of brain organoids has
been evaluating the developmental neurotoxicity of drugs and environmental toxins. Rodent and animal models poorly predict human neurotoxicity,[20] as brains develop quite diﬀerently across
species.[21] Traditional in vitro models utilizing 2D cultures of
immortalized cell lines also have limited functional capacities.
In contrast, the structured 3D human neural tissue of organoids
allows the assessment of toxicants on processes like progenitor
proliferation, neuronal diﬀerentiation, migration, and network
formation during development[22,23] (Figure 2).
Studies have evaluated organoid responses to diverse neurotoxicants. For example, the antidepressant paroxetine impairs neural progenitor proliferation.[24] Environmental toxins like methylmercury, arsenic, and cadmium alter neural
diﬀerentiation.[25–27] Rotenone treatment led to higher levels
of reactive oxygen species, impaired neurite outgrowth, and
synaptogenesis.[28]

3.1. Mixture Toxicity and Interaction Studies
Most toxicology research has focused on eﬀects of individual
chemicals. However, real-world exposures often involve mixtures of toxicants that may exert synergistic interactions. Brain
organoids allow controlled exposure to deﬁned mixtures and
combinations to determine interactions that are diﬃcult to assess
in vivo. For example, organoids demonstrated gene-environment
interactions between chlorpyrifos pesticide and CHD8 autism
risk gene mutations, validating epidemiological associations.[17]
Gene environment interactions are boosted by stem-cell-derived
models in combination with molecular biology techniques such
as CRISPR/Cas allowing to introduce or repair risk genes.[32,33]
Our ongoing studies are examining combined eﬀects of heavy
metal mixtures like arsenic, cadmium, and chromium [unpublished]. Organoids provide new opportunities to model real-world
mixture exposures and genetic susceptibilities relevant to human
risk assessment.

4.1. Modeling Neurological Diseases with Brain Organoids
Brain organoids provide a human experimental system to model
aspects of neurological diseases and insight into their underlying mechanisms.[34–37] For neurodevelopmental disorders
like autism spectrum disorders (ASD), organoids with patientderived mutations have replicated disease features indicating altered neurogenesis with the gene of Forkhead Box protein G1
(FOXG1) overexpression.[38] Organoids from ASD patients have
shown disrupted cortical lamination among other phenotypes.[39]

4.2. Modeling Neuroinfectious Diseases with Brain Organoids
Organoids are uniquely suited to model infections of humanspeciﬁc pathogens that are inaccessible in animal models.[50–52]
Key advantages of organoids over standard models include a 3D
human neural environment, patient-derived genomes, and evolutionary conserved pathways absent in animal models.[53] Limitations exist, such as lack of vasculature, BBB, and peripheral
immune components; immaturity of cells[54] also diﬀers from
adult infections. However, organoids provide unprecedented access to study neuroinvasive pathogens in a human setting. Brain
organoids have been leveraged to demonstrate neurotropism
and examine mechanisms of diverse viruses with neurological
impacts, including Zika,[55,56] cytomegalovirus,[57] herpes simplex virus,[53,58,59] human iummndeﬁciency virus (HIV),[60,61]
Dengue,[62] measles,[63] johns cunningham (JC) virus,[64] and
severe acute respiratory syndrome corona virus- 2 (SARS-CoV2).[65–67] For example, they allowed to study eﬀects on neurodevelopment, such as Cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection disrupting calcium signaling and causing structural abnormalities.[68,69]
Organoids have been used to screen and validate antiviral compounds, as done for Zika virus.[70]
Microglia can be added to assess neuroinﬂammation,[62] while
introducing blood vessel compartments allows studying eﬀects
on the blood-brain barrier.[71] Cerebral organoids have to the best
of our knowledge not yet been applied to model bacterial infections. Recently, Seo and colleagues developed an in vitro model
for T. gondii infection using human brain organoids (HBOs).[72]
Compared to immortalized cell lines, organoids better mimic
complex human tissue environments that inﬂuence infection
and pathology. The addition of micro-glia enables also the study
of immune responses.[60]
Challenges exist given organoids’ fetal state diﬀers from adult
brains aﬀected in most infections. However, organoids provide
a platform to model infectious mechanisms and conduct drug
screens not previously possible for human-speciﬁc pathogens.

4.3. Personalized Medicine
Brain organoids allow the study of neurological diseases and
testing of therapies in a personalized manner using cells derived from individual patients (Figure 1).[74] This enables drug
screening on patient-speciﬁc organoids before treating the patient, providing a platform for “clinical trials in a dish.” For example, organoids have been generated from glioblastoma patients’
tumor cells and used to screen cancer drugs to determine the
most promising options before initiating treatment.[14] Stem-cellderived neurons from a Timothy syndrome patient’s iPSCs exhibited corresponding neuronal hyperexcitability that was rescued
by roscovitine, validating a potential therapy.[75] Similar opportunities are seen with brain organoids in ASD.[76]
Brain organoids could eventually enable routine testing of
therapies on patient-derived models to determine optimal personalized approaches. Though still early, organoids present opportunities to tailor medicine and improve clinical outcomes that
are unprecedented with traditional methods. Challenges remain
in time to the availability of models for individual patient decisions, adult-like maturation, and validation of organoid-based assays, but precision medicine approaches are clearly on the horizon.

5.2. Validation of (Brain) MPS
Validation is critical for regulatory acceptance and broader
adoption of MPS as predictive tools in biomedicine and
toxicology.[80,81] However, traditional validation frameworks have
limitations when applied to complex, mechanism-driven technologies like MPS.[82] New ﬁt-for-purpose approaches are needed
to focus on performance standards and mechanistic relevance.[83]
“Mechanistic validation” evaluates how well the biological mechanisms modeled align with current scientiﬁc knowledge from literature review and human pathophysiology. This systems biology
approach is better suited for MPS than validating against animal
models with unclear human translatability.
Deﬁning “ﬁt-for-purpose” means aligning MPS design and
validation to the speciﬁc use-case and information needs.[72]
MPS developed to model neurodevelopmental processes warrant diﬀerent validation criteria than systems modeling liver
metabolism. Purpose drives validation strategy. “Performance
standards” involve deﬁning engineering speciﬁcations and quantitative benchmarks that MPS must satisfy as measures of
readiness.[84,85] These could encompass bioprinted architecture,
cell viability, tissue function, endpoint dynamic range, and response concordance with reference data. Overall, ﬁt-for-purpose
validation based on performance standards and mechanism establishes conﬁdence in MPS as useful tools versus reproducing animal tests. Precompetitive collaborations are key to building stakeholder consensus on appropriate MPS validation frameworks. Quality assurance via initiatives such as Good Cell Culture
Practice 2.0 also underpin successful validation.[86]

5.1. Current limitations
It is important to note that while both brain organoids and brainspheres provide valuable models for studying the human brain,
they are not perfect replicas. They may lack certain cell subtypes,
do not have all brain regions, and lack sensory input from a
“body.” Furthermore, they may not fully replicate the complex circuitry needed to model complex brain diseases or normal cognition. In many aspects, they are immature reﬂecting fetal stages of
the human brain, which can aﬀect their ability to accurately represent adult brain tissue. Brain organoids may not contain all the
cell types found in the human brain, which can limit their ability
to accurately model brain function and disease. The physiology
of brain organoids may not accurately reﬂect that of the human
brain, which can limit their reliability for certain applications,
and does not accurately represent the spatial organization of different cell types and brain regions found in the human brain.[77]

6. The Future of Brain Organoids
6.1. Integrating Brain Organoids with Emerging Technologies
Brain organoids provide an unprecedented platform to model human neurobiology in vitro.[88] However, realizing their full potential requires integration with other rapidly advancing technologies. This multi-disciplinary approach enables novel applications
at the intersection of organoid biology and leading-edge bioengineering.
6.1.1. Genomics and Personalized Medicine
Deriving organoids from patient-speciﬁc iPSCs allows the incorporation of genetic backgrounds into disease modeling (see
above). Patient-derived organoids oﬀer prospects for the selection of individual therapies.[89] Linking organoids with clinical “-omics” data through biobanks facilitates personalized
medicine.[90] Organoids could screen drug responses tailored to
an individual’s genome prior to prescribing.[91]
6.1.2. CRISPR and Gene Editing
Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats
(CRISPR) gene editing alters organoid genomes to introduce disease mutations or ﬂuorescent reporters.[92,93] It enables rapid iteration of genetic variants for phenotypic screening in organoids.
Gene-edited organoids can elucidate genotype-phenotype relationships and gene function.
6.1.3. Artiﬁcial Intelligence
High-throughput imaging of organoids generates complex multidimensional data. Artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) techniques like
deep learning are being applied to extract insights from organoid
models.[94] Machine learning can help classify cell types, identify
morphological features, and detect functional patterns. The combination of AI with brain organoids opens up a new ﬁeld of OI
(see below), establishing memory and learning in a dish as called
for in the Baltimore Declaration toward OI.[95]
6.1.4. Microbiome
The microbiome inﬂuences neurodevelopment and CNS
function.[96] Co-culturing organoids with microbes or their products can help unravel these interactions. Integrating microbiome
and immune components makes multi-organ-on-chip systems
more representative models.

6.2. Computational Systems Biology and Toxicology
Systems biology applies computational modeling to integrate
complex biological data and understand emergent functions and
behaviors.[107,108] Coupled with high-throughput experiments, it
provides a powerful framework for tackling biomedicine’s complexity versus traditional reductionism.[109] Systems toxicology
applies these approaches to model chemical perturbations and
adverse outcomes.[110,111]
Recent examples showcase systems biology’s utility:
-Modeling signaling networks and dynamics elucidated how the
order and timing of anti-cancer drugs impact combinatorial
eﬃcacy.[112]
-Paradoxical activation of RAF/MAPK signaling by certain kinase inhibitors was explained by feedback loops and allosteric
mechanisms.[113,114]
-Virtual Tissue models applying Tox21/ToxCast data simulated
chemical impacts on vascular development.[115,116]
-Agent-based modeling of cleft palate formation elucidated key
developmental toxicity mechanisms.[117]
Systems biology is still rarely combined with MPS experimentation, but tremendous opportunities exist.[118] As simpler yet
functional models of human biology, MPS are ideal systems to
capture multi-parametric data for computational modeling.[119]
Such fusion can help unravel MPS complexities and scale results to the in vivo level via quantitative in vitro–in vivo extrapolation (QIVIVE).[120,121] Overall, the synergistic application of systems biology computational models with high-content MPS experiments oﬀers an exciting path to tackling outstanding problems in biomedical research and toxicology.

6.3. Future Outlook: Organoid-AI Integration
An emerging frontier is combining organoids with artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) systems in what has been termed “Organoid
Intelligence.”[122–124] The goal is to utilize stem cell-derived
organoids not just as models, but as active components integrated into biohybrid systems exhibiting cognition and learning.
Early examples have coupled neuronal cultures with robots in
simple biohybrid systems.[125] Cortical Labs has developed DishBrain, a system that achieved rudimentary learning of computer
games using networked neuronal cultures.[126,127] Eﬀorts are underway to integrate brain organoids with AI to model higher cognition not possible in either system alone.
While still aspirational, OI has parallels to early artiﬁcial intelligence research, which started in the 1950s before the technology existed to implement it.[128] OI proponents have outlined a roadmap to incrementally realize this vision,[118,119] which
includes improving organoid design, sensorimotor integration,
electrophysiology analysis, and closed-loop feedback between
organoid and AI components. If achieved, OI systems could provide revolutionary new platforms to study and understand the
human brain.
OI oﬀers unprecedented opportunities to elucidate mechanisms in neurophysiology and advance pharmacology and toxicology relevant to the brain. By recapitulating human brain cell
composition[129] and increasing architecture, OI systems permit
direct experimental access to study processes underlying neuronal signaling and network activity. As OI models incorporate
greater cell complexity, connectivity, and myelination approaching human brains, the platforms promise increasingly faithful representations of cognition and higher functions. Already
early OI proof-of-concept studies demonstrate responsiveness to
stimuli and learning capabilities mediated by neurophysiological
changes.[127,130] The future integration of microﬂuidics, biosensors, and electronics will further enhance experimental control
and measurement resolution. On the pharmacological side, OI
potentially represents a breakthrough technology for central nervous system drug development and toxicological safety testing.
The human-speciﬁc ontogeny enables direct modeling of neurological diseases while avoiding misleading animal-to-human
translatability gaps. Patient-derived OI could facilitate personalized medicine approaches. Combined with machine learning analytics, high-throughput OI screening presents a path to transform the discovery of neuroactive compounds. Overall, OI heralds a new era in neurosciences and brain-targeted pharmacology
where human experimental models play a central role.
OI holds also intriguing potential to inspire new directions
in neuromorphic engineering and pioneer unprecedented biological computing capabilities. As research elucidates how brain
organoids exhibit learning and information processing grounded
in dynamic neuronal signaling, these ﬁndings could guide the
development of hardware and algorithms emulating biological
neural networks. For example, key mechanisms enabling eﬃcient data representation, adaptation, and cognition in OI systems may be translated into enhanced machine-learning architectures. Beyond Software AI, living OI networks also introduce
prospects for entirely novel wetware computing substrates. If
scaled organoid assemblies can recreate higher-order functions
rivaling brains, they may interface with electronic systems to

6.4. Convergence of Brain Organoids and Brain-Computer
Interfaces
Brain organoids derived from stem cells allow the modeling of
human brain development and disorders in vitro. As described
above, these 3D culture systems self-organize to exhibit features like layered architecture, diverse neural cell types, synaptogenesis, and myelination. In parallel, brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) are emerging as a transformative technology allowing direct communication between the brain and external devices. BCIs aim to restore sensory-motor functions for paralysis
through brain implants, but also have potential for memory augmentation, knowledge access, and seamless thought-based control of technologies.
The convergence of these two disruptive ﬁelds—human brain
modeling via organoids and brain-machine integration via BCI—
could unlock new possibilities. Organoids could inform safer BCI
design by replicating neural anatomy and physiology. BCI sensors could analyze organoid activity patterns during development
and disease modeling.
Most provocatively, combining organoids with BCI could enable advanced OI systems exhibiting human-like learning and
memory (see above). This could revolutionize brain research, education, and human-AI symbiosis. However, such possibilities
also raise major ethical concerns regarding human enhancement
and testbed organoid sentience that require careful oversight. In
summary, the synergistic combination of organoids and BCI constitutes an important area for neuroscience and bioengineering
research, but one requiring ethical foresight to ensure responsible innovation. It exempliﬁes the technological convergence reshaping biomedicine.

research mainly tried to model physiology and diseases, there

as a type of synthetic biology. Realizing a “hyper-physiological”
organoid system could push boundaries on multiple fronts
(Figure 3).

6.5.2. Unbound to Embryogenesis
Mimicking embryogenesis is useful for studying it and its derailment in disease and developmental neurotoxicity. But these

-High-throughput toxicology screening platforms
-Patient-speciﬁc therapeutic testing for precision medicine
-Public biobanks integrating organoids with clinical and genomic

-Commercialization for drug development and diagnostics
6.6.3. Convergence with Emerging Technologies

6.5.3. Synthetic Biology and Novel Cell Sources
This engineering perspective is unconstrained by evolution. Synthetic biology approaches could engineer organoids for example
with expanded cognitive capabilities or protective mechanisms
against pathology. The cell’s genetic make-up can be altered by
molecular biology allowing or inhibiting certain biological responses, for example, by knock-outs or reporter genes but also
the expression of receptors. Organoids do not need to be restricted to a single genetic background. Incorporating cells from
multiple donors, for example, gifted Asperger children or other
cell types with speciﬁc genetic traits, could create enhanced systems drawing on diverse natural capabilities. Hybrids between
donors, with sentinel cells carrying certain reporter genes or even
chimera with animal cells are possible as the brain organoids do
not have an immune system.

6.5.4. Novel Inputs and Sensors
Inputs like optogenetics enable manipulating and monitoring
organoid activity with high spatiotemporal precision.[132] Interfacing organoids with biosensors and electronics facilitates analyzing their form and function.[133]
In summary, embracing ambitious bioengineering goals could
transform organoids into hyper-physiological systems with capabilities exceeding natural embryonic development. This requires
an integrated, multi-disciplinary approach at the cutting edge of
stem cell biology, tissue engineering, and emerging technologies.

6.6.7. Investment Prioritization
Given their disruption potential, brain organoids warrant prioritized research funding. Public-private partnerships and coordinating major initiatives would accelerate biomedical translation.
Models, which represent industry standards, are needed to collaboratively leverage these technologies.
In summary, realizing the promise of brain organoids to transform neuroscience and its application for drug development and
public health requires embracing ambitious goals across scientiﬁc, computing, medical, and ethical domains through continued creativity and global collaboration. Their future directions
carry both tremendous opportunities and responsibilities.

7. Conclusions
Brain organoids allow neuroscience studies not previously possible using traditional techniques. Their unique advantages include human settings, patient-derived genomes, emerging protocols to enhance control and direct diﬀerentiation, and amenability to gene editing. Limitations exist, including variability, fetallike states, and lack of sensory inputs or vascularization. However, organoids have already yielded insights into human brain
development, neurological disorders, infections, toxicology, and
personalized medicine.
OI constitutes an aspirational future direction to integrate
organoids with AI to generate advanced models of learning and
memory. Though still an emerging ﬁeld, brain organoids have
the disruptive potential to transform drug development, disease
modeling, toxicology testing, and our basic understanding of the
human brain. Continued research and innovation in this area is
critical to fully realize these possibilities.
